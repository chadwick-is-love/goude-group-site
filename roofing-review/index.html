01 · HOW ACCESS WORKS

Four rules, before the list.

The Proposal promises that every account is in Roofing Company's name, and that if we ever stop working together, nothing switches off and no password changes hands. These four rules are how that stays true from the first day.

In Roofing Company's name

Every account in this addendum is opened by Roofing Company, in Roofing Company's name, and paid by Roofing Company directly. Where we set one up, we set it up with you, and the account is yours.

Invited, never given a password

We are added as users, the way you would add a new hire. Taking us off is one step for whoever holds the account, and it changes nothing for anyone else.

Nothing that moves money

Every build reads, flags and drafts. We do not need access to the bank or to payroll, and we will not ask for it. Where we connect to the accounting system, nothing we build can pay, file or submit.

One holder per account

Each account has one person at Roofing Company who holds its main login. Every account in this addendum goes on the account register in the Operating Review, so Roofing Company always knows what it has and who holds it.

PART 1 · 05 · LEADERSHIP AND OWNERSHIP

Rules that hold only while you are watching them.

What we found. Every six to eight months the review cadence slips unnoticed and becomes a backlog that has reached twenty deals, then fifteen, then thirty. The last one cost two to three weeks of senior time to clear. Every large deal waits on your approval check, most of which is mechanical. A thousand old takeoffs sit on stale prices. The out-of-state tax field sometimes fails silently on seven figure contracts. Scouting is the biggest single block in your month and it is done by eye in Google Earth. Repeat questions that belong in a written answer interrupt you through the day. The gap. The company has good rules and no mechanism. You are the mechanism. The fix. Move the watching from a person to a screen. Stuck deals, stale prices and tax logic get flagged automatically and you review the exceptions. Ninety percent of your pricing already lives in one Everseal sheet, so most of this is connecting what exists rather than inventing anything.

What it returns. Your attention, and an end to a multi-week clear-up that has already happened at least twice.

PART 1 · 06 · SALES AND ESTIMATING

Two senior sellers spend four days a week checking arithmetic.

What we found. Takeoffs are done by hand against the Everseal chart, and two of your leaders named this separately as the most valuable fix in the company. Four of the five review steps are mechanical. Proposals are made by finding a similar old packet in Drive and changing the name, address, square footage, date and price. Diagrams are drawn from scratch before every inspection, and missing photos in CompanyCam mean return trips. Inbound leads have a one hour callback rule that nothing enforces, on ten to fifteen percent of your volume. Repeat and referral work is a quarter or more of all leads and nobody resurfaces it on a schedule. The Everseal sheet runs to about a thousand products and moves once or twice a year. The gap. PART 2 · 17 · FINDING THE NEXT ROOF

The scouting question, answered with data.

Sam's map shows every roof Roofing Company is on. Reed's question is which roof to knock on next. The signal is public record, and it stops at the state line.

Harper County publishes every building permit. 74,502 records from January 2011 to the present, refreshed monthly, with parcel ID, address, work description, dollar value and, on most, the name of the contractor who did the work. 6,444 of them are on commercial property, and 388 of those mention roofing work, 351 of them with the contractor named. Free, with attribution. We re-ran these counts on September 21. Two lists fall out of one query. Every commercial roof re-done recently, which Roofing Company should stop knocking on. Every commercial building with no roofing permit in fifteen years, against Roofing Company's own fifteen to twenty year roof life. Plus competitor activity by name and dollar value, monthly. It is Millbrook only. Harper County ran 7,123 permits in the last twelve months, checked on September 22, 2026. No other county in the territory is in this dataset. Harlow's cities run three different portals with no data feed. Pinecrest takes permit applications by email. No statewide Mississippi database exists.

The paid databases do not fix it. One charges $XXX to $XXX a month for data Harper gives away. The bid-stage services miss re-roofs by design. Imagery can. Free federal aerial imagery is flown roughly every three years, too slow to catch a re-roof. One commercial provider claims roof age on 151 million parcels, residential and commercial, blending imagery change detection with permits and assessor records. Pricing and rural Mississippi capture frequency are unpublished. That quote is the one question that decides the Mississippi half.

What we would do

Millbrook first, at zero data cost: a one week proof that puts the two lists on Reed's map. Mississippi after the quote. If the imagery provider covers rural counties at a sane price, it beats permits outright. If not, we say so plainly: Millbrook is solvable for free, and north Mississippi is feet and relationships. It sits on the twelve month horizon, once the first ninety days are running.

PART 3 · 05 · THE SYSTEM EVERYTHING SITS ON

Build 1 of 1490 daysIn the Roofing Company app

The Roofing Company app, the job record and its five views.

One record for every job, from signed to warrantied. Everything else in this book writes to it or reads from it. It is the only item here that is not a point fix.

Horizon

First ninety days. Committed. Nothing after it starts until it passes its test.

Why it exists

Your operations coordinator built a version of this himself, in an afternoon, and showed it to us on screen. Active, not started and completed pins on a map, with roof type, square footage and building type on each. It stopped working when the map provider changed its terms and started asking for a key.

here's, like, a little map that I made using, I think I used Claude for this.

Walt asked for the same thing more than once, independently. Ownership asked for a dashboard in the same breath as apologizing for asking, because it did not sound like AI to him. It is the one item three people requested without being prompted and without knowing the others had.

Why it is not another app to log into. A task app was tried here and abandoned. The reason given was not that it lacked features. "there's just a lot of tabs and choices and things. It needs to be, like, very few choices, very few options, straight to the point." The measured budget for a field interaction is thirty seconds, and that is the design constraint, not a target.

How it works

A signed deal in your sales pipeline creates the job. Nobody types it twice, and the sales pipeline keeps everything up to the signature. The record carries the roof, the system type, the square footage, the address, the dates, the equipment on site, the milestones and the closeout state. Three taps a day from whoever is on the job: crew on site, equipment checked, anything blocking. A fourth appears only when a milestone is reached. The taps queue on the phone when there is no signal and send when there is. Roofs and basements drop coverage and the count of a fourteen day test is taps made, not taps received. Five views read the same record. A company view for ownership, a map for Walt, a today list for project managers, a billing view for finance, a caller view for the front office. Four rules run on it: sale to job handoff, milestone to billing prompt, closeout verification, and an accountability alert when something sits past its expected time.

What it reads and writes

Your sales pipeline at contract signed, one direction. CompanyCam for the photo and completion record, which stays exactly as it is. Your accounting system for the billing milestone. The connection hub runs on an account in your name, so when a vendor changes something the vendor maintains the connector rather than us.

Where you see it

A page on a phone or a browser. What the field sees is three buttons. Nobody types a paragraph into it. The only other new thing the field opens is the receipt app, which comes with the expense card rather than with a build.

What is inside the Roofing Company app

One page on a phone or a browser, for the people running jobs. The daily check: three taps, crew on site, equipment checked, anything blocking. A fourth tap when a milestone is reached, which reaches finance as a billing prompt the same day. Flag an issue. Percent complete, one tap, which becomes Roofing Company's own production rate. Closeout: a job cannot close until the equipment is back and the punch list is done. Every tap is saved on the phone with no signal and sends when coverage returns. Photos stay in CompanyCam, receipts in the receipt app and calls on the phone line, so none of them move into it.

Who opens it. Operations and the project managers see today, ownership sees the company, Walt sees the map, finance sees what to bill and the front office sees the caller. The head of sales never needs to, because a signed deal creates the job. Six of the fourteen builds live in it as they arrive: this one, shortfall alerts, weather and client dates, warranty and callback triage, change orders caught on the roof and the production rate. Walt's field manual writes into it. The other seven stay in the tools the team already uses.

What has to be true

Everything before contract signed stays in your sales pipeline. This record owns signed to paid. One connection between them, one direction. It is what makes leaving any of this later a small job rather than a migration. What the field sees works with no signal. Not negotiable, and it is the single most likely reason a fourteen day test fails for a reason nobody reads correctly. The three taps stay three. Every field added to them is paid for by someone standing on a roof. It has to be faster than the text message it replaces on day one.

What it will not do

It does not pay, file or submit anything. It reads, flags and drafts. It does not replace CompanyCam and it does not ask the crews to move off it. It does not report on a named person, and the alerts are addressed to the person who can act, not about the person who did not.

How we will know

A project manager completes the daily check on a live job for fourteen straight days, and the operations coordinator has not had to call anyone to find out where the equipment is.

PART 3 · 06 · 90 DAYS / TAKEOFF

Build 2 of 1490 days

The takeoff calculator.

The measurements come back from the roof and the material list, the quantities and the sheet fill themselves in, with the material order on a second tab.

Horizon

First ninety days. Committed.

Why it exists

The head of sales raised this three separate times in one hour without being asked, and answered yes twice when asked directly whether the arithmetic is done by hand.

It's just the measurements. We gotta go through and add everything up, we gotta figure out how much termination we need, fasteners, things like that. It's doing all the math.

Ownership independently called it worth the whole engagement on its own. Two people, two separate conversations, no coordination. Only the job record has more.

Why nothing off the shelf does it

We searched the commercial estimating market for three calculations together: roll goods with overlap and waste, fastener counts against the attachment pattern, and curb flashings off the Everseal chart. No product was verified to do all three. AccuLynx, Dataforma and Jobba were each checked and each fails on scope or on fit. There is no buyable layer between a good spreadsheet and a ten thousand dollar platform you configure yourself.

How it works

The field form arrives with the roof dimensions, the deck type, the layer count and the penetration list. The sheet picks the membrane from the system chosen, then calculates roll goods against that membrane's real width, which runs 2 feet 8 inches, 5 feet or 5 feet 4 inches depending on product, and applies the overlap and waste factors already in your sheet. Fasteners are calculated against the attachment pattern for the deck type and wind rating, not a flat rate per square. Each curb is looked up against the Everseal size chart rather than estimated, which is the step that currently requires a person to open a PDF. Termination bar, boots, drip edge and starter come off the linear measurements. The material order writes to a second tab in the format the supplier expects.

What it reads and writes

Your existing Google Sheets takeoff template and its formulas. The Everseal curb chart behind your contractor portal login. The Everseal price sheet.

Where you see it

Your own Google Sheet. No new system, no login.

What has to be true

Your sheet's formulas can be read and reproduced. Ownership confirmed on screen that they are built in, and offered the template. The curb chart can be transcribed into a lookup once. It changes rarely. It beats your estimator doing it by hand on day one, on a real job. Not negotiable, and it is the reason Estimating Edge failed here.

What it will not do

It does not set a price and it does not send anything. It produces quantities. A person prices and a person approves.

How we will know

Stopwatch, one real job, against the same person doing it by hand. Today the takeoff runs one to two hours before any review. Two things could reduce this work and neither has been checked. Everseal factory-fabricates curb flashings from contractor measurements, so ask your rep what takeoff support comes with an order. And Holcim Elevate publishes a free wind calculator that generates fastening patterns.

PART 3 · 07 · 6 MONTHS / PRE-CHECK

Build 3 of 146 months

Deal review pre-check.

Before a deal reaches a reviewer, the mechanical checks have already run, so the reviewer reads the proposal instead of re-adding the numbers.

Horizon

Months four to six. Directional. Narrowed to the checks your sales software cannot do, and it waits on two things: the HubSpot tier confirmed, and an hour with the first reviewer in the approval chain.

Why it exists

Ownership walked the approval live on screen. It is eight steps in a fixed order, after a five step review by the head of sales. Four of the five review steps are mechanical. Only reading the proposal takes judgment. On why the process drifts:

I'm setting some stuff up, and it's half-baked, and then I don't hold the line with my leadership.

And on the consequence, which recurs: "every 6 to 8 months I have to revisit this conversation and retrain." Each drift became a backlog: twenty deals, then fifteen, then thirty. What is a build and what is not. The required fields themselves are built into HubSpot at Sales Hub Professional, and is listed in this book under what you already own. What is built is the arithmetic that runs before a deal can move forward. No product reconciles a Google Sheets takeoff against a HubSpot deal.

How it works

A deal moves into the approval stage in HubSpot. The check pulls the linked takeoff sheet and the CompanyCam project. It reconciles four things: the square footage on the proposal against the takeoff, the takeoff total against the deal value, the presence of the required photos, and the price per square against the range for that system and tear-off depth. Anything that fails is written as a note on the deal and posted to the review channel in Slack, naming the specific line rather than a generic failure. Anything that passes is marked clear, and the reviewer opens it knowing the arithmetic already holds.

What it reads and writes

HubSpot deal records, the Google Sheets takeoff, CompanyCam photos, and a Slack channel.

Where you see it

A note on the HubSpot deal, and a message in Slack.

What has to be true

Roofing Company is on Sales Hub Professional, or moves to it. Below that tier there are no required fields for this to sit in front of, and the build gets larger. The four checks are expressible as rules. Supported by the walkthrough, which was strikingly mechanical. The reviewers accept the pre-check rather than re-doing it anyway. Habit is the risk here, not technology.

What it will not do

It does not approve anything. It runs the four mechanical checks and hands a person the one that needs judgment.

How we will know

Approvals cleared per hour on a review day, before and after. And the backlog that cost two to three weeks should not recur at month seven. Still open, and it matters to this build specifically. The first reviewer in the approval chain has not been interviewed. Every design decision here assumes how he works.

PART 3 · 08 · 90 DAYS / DUPLICATE PAYMENTS

Build 4 of 1490 days

The ledger checked against the bank, every day, before and after February.

A client who has already paid gets a collection email every two days. It has happened to a real one. This is the build that stops it, and it has a deadline that is not ours.

Horizon

First ninety days. Committed, and it keeps running after the February cutover. Sage has no check of its own for a customer payment recorded twice.

Why it exists

Finance raised it four times, in writing and in conversation, without being prompted. Two customer checks go onto one deposit slip, so the bank shows one credit. The accounting system cannot match one credit to two invoices, so it records the payment as not made. The invoice stays open, the automated reminder fires at a client who has already paid, and finance applies it by hand to stop that. The manual entry and the feed entry can then both exist, which is the duplicate. That is one chain, not three problems, and the detectable event is the unmatched combined deposit on the day it lands rather than the duplicate months later. And there is a free answer that goes in front of this build. Turn the automatic reminders off and chase deliberately. Overdue invoices are already chased by phone because the emails land in spam. That stops a paying client being chased for payment this week, at no cost, and it is worth doing whether or not this gets built.

How it works

The bank is read daily and matched against open invoices in a short window. Three lists come out: money in but not recorded, invoices about to be chased where the money appears to have arrived, and entries recorded twice. One email to finance. Nothing is applied, voided, written off or sent. It looks only at open invoices from the last few weeks, not the whole ledger, so it stays fast and quiet.

What it reads and writes

Your bank, read only, and your accounting ledger, read only. It writes an email and nothing else.

Where you see it

One message a day, in the inbox finance already works from.

What has to be true

The bank allows an automated read. If it does not, the same check runs from an exported statement and the build gets slightly less convenient rather than impossible. It is written against the bank and the job, not the ledger. The bank does not change in February. That is what turns the cutover into about a day of rework rather than a rebuild, and it is the reason building this before February is not wasted.

What it will not do

It does not pay, void, write off or chase anybody for payment. It does not stop the accounting system sending its own reminder, which is why the free fix above comes first.

The cleanup is separate, and it is not conditional

Whatever is wrong in the ledger today crosses into the new system as opening balances in February, and the duplicates surface at tax time as undeclared deposits. Clearing them is part of this build: we do the work, your CPA signs the result in January and your conversion partner loads it, and its deadline is the cutover rather than ours. How large that clearing is gets set once we have seen one real example, and getting it costs nothing: one combined deposit, the bank line, the invoices it should have cleared, what the system did and what finance did by hand. It settles whether this is a matching failure or genuine duplication, and how noisy the matching will be.

How we will know

No client who has paid receives a collection notice, and the count of duplicate entries found per month is a number somebody can say out loud.

PART 3 · 09 · 90 DAYS / TAX AND LIEN

Build 5 of 1490 days

State tax and lien notice flag.

Each state's tax rule and each state's notice deadlines are checked on every takeoff, and flagged beside the price.

Horizon

First ninety days. Committed.

Why it exists

Walking a real approval on screen, ownership noticed the tax field was not populating on a Mississippi job and said so mid-sentence. Mississippi requires contractors over a certain size to pay three and a half percent up front. It fails silently, it failed in front of us, and it fails on a class of contract that runs into seven figures.

Why it is a build

Mississippi's contractor withholding and multi-state lien deadlines run off the same two facts on the same record: which state, and which date. Nobody sells this for a commercial roofer working Tennessee, Mississippi, Arkansas and the occasional out of state project.

How it works

The takeoff carries a project address, which resolves to a state. The state selects a rule. Mississippi applies the three and a half percent contractor withholding against the job total. Other states apply their own or none. The flag sits beside the price rather than in a hidden cell, so an unpopulated rule is visible rather than silent.

The same address and the contract date drive the lien calendar: preliminary notice, notice of intent and filing deadlines per state, surfaced as dates rather than as a document to go and read. Anything approaching a deadline surfaces before it passes.

What it reads and writes

The Google Sheets takeoff. Nothing else.

Where you see it

A flag beside the price, in the sheet you are already in.

What has to be true

The rules can be listed for your real territory, which spans at least four states. Somebody owns keeping them current when a state changes a rate. If the answer is nobody, this build carries a review date and we will say so rather than let it quietly age.

What it will not do

It does not file anything and it does not submit anything. It flags dates and amounts. A person acts on them.

How we will know

Count of contracts where the tax field failed silently. Today at least one, found by accident, in a meeting. The lien half was proposed separately and is folded in here rather than priced twice, because the hard part is the state lookup and it is shared. One piece of work protects both tax exposure and collection rights.

PART 3 · 10 · ROOFING COMPANY'S TO DO / SECURITY

Roofing Company's to do

Two-step sign-in, and a rule before money moves.

Not a build in the usual sense. It is the shortest distance between the thing that already happened to you and it not happening again.

Horizon

First ninety days. Not a build: this one is Roofing Company's to do, not ours. Committed. It is in the first phase because the exposure is live now, not because it is difficult.

Why it exists

A phishing email compromised a mailbox this year. The attacker posed as a client and pushed fraudulent payment activity. Reporting it locked the owner out of payroll for a day and two hours went on writing checks by hand. That is one incident, already survived, and it is the reason this is not a hypothetical page. Ownership described it plainly in the interview as something that happened rather than something feared. The Operating Review scores security at twenty out of twenty-five, the joint second highest exposure in the company, and this is most of what closes it.

How it works

Two-step sign-in is enforced on every mailbox in the company, not offered. It is included in the license you already pay for. A written callback rule: any request to change payment details, bank details or a remit-to address is confirmed by calling the number already on file, never the number in the email. The domain is authenticated so mail claiming to be from Roofing Company can be checked by the receiving server, which also fixes invoices landing in spam. Every person who leaves has their access removed the same week, from a list that exists.

What it reads and writes

The admin side of your company email account, and your domain records. Nothing else, and it writes nothing into any financial system.

Where you see it

You do not. It is configuration and one written rule on one page, which the front office and finance both hold.

What has to be true

Somebody at Roofing Company owns the admin side of the company email account and can enforce a policy on it. If that access sits with a departed contractor, that is the first thing to fix. The callback rule is honored when it is inconvenient, which is the only time it matters.

What it will not do

It does not stop a convincing email arriving. It stops the arriving email being enough on its own to move money.

How we will know

Two-step is on for every mailbox, the callback rule is written and the finance lead can recite it, and the domain passes an authentication check from outside.
