Payrolls went negative in July. Price both Septembers before the Fed picks one.

What moved

The July jobs report landed Friday: the economy shed 23,000 jobs against forecasts of an 80,000 gain, and May and June were revised down a combined 103,000. Unemployment ticked down to 4.1 percent, and annual wage growth slowed to 3.2 percent. Markets repriced within the hour: the quarter-point rate increase that had been the September base case became the less likely outcome, with the Fed's decision due September 16.

What it changes

Two operator facts moved at once. Hiring: the market loosened, retail cut 19,400 jobs and private payrolls barely grew, so candidates who were unreachable in spring answer the phone now, and wage pressure is easing. Borrowing: the increase lenders were pricing into September lost its majority, which steadies every floating-rate credit line, SBA loan, and equipment note you carry. Cheaper money is not here; more expensive money got less likely.

What not to do

Do not read one negative month as a recession and freeze hiring you already budgeted; a loose market is exactly when good people become reachable. Do not rush to lock a fixed rate out of fear this week, because the pressure pushing rates up just eased. And do not let September 16 pass unwatched: if you carry floating-rate debt, that afternoon prices it either way, and you should know your exposure to the quarter point before the announcement.

The tape

Aug 7The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports July payrolls at minus 23,000 against expectations near plus 80,000. Unemployment falls to 4.1 percent. Average annual earnings growth slows to 3.2 percent, under the 3.5 percent estimate.
RevisionsMay drops from 129,000 to 63,000 and June from 57,000 to 20,000, a combined 103,000 fewer jobs than previously reported. Against expectations, the total July shortfall runs roughly a quarter million positions.
SectorsPrivate payrolls add 30,000 against 78,000 expected. Government sheds 53,000, retail 19,400, financial activities 14,000. Healthcare adds 22,000 and manufacturing 5,000. The customer-facing economy is where the cutting is.
Sept 16The fed funds range sits at 3.50 to 3.75 percent. Before the report, futures priced a 55 percent chance of a quarter-point increase at the September meeting; after it, the odds flipped to 56 percent for holding steady. Not a cut. A reprieve.

A loose market is a quiet opening

The instinct on a bad jobs number is defensive, and for hiring it should be the opposite. The roles you could not fill in March, when every candidate had three offers, are fillable in a market that just shed retail and office jobs by the tens of thousands. Wage growth at 3.2 percent means the counter-offer math has moved in your favor for the first time in years. None of that requires a prediction about recessions; it only requires noticing who answers the ad this month versus five months ago.

The borrowing side is about knowing one number. A quarter point on your floating-rate debt is a specific dollar figure, and most owners have never written it down; they carry the anxiety instead of the arithmetic. Price it once with the instrument on the right and September 16 becomes a calendar entry instead of a cliff. If the Fed holds, nothing changes. If it moves, you already knew the cost to the dollar.

The play

  1. Reopen the one or two roles you shelved in spring. Run the ads for two weeks and count who answers now; the market did the recruiting-budget work for you.
  2. List every floating-rate obligation: the credit line, the SBA 7(a), equipment notes, any card balance that rides. Mark which ones reprice with prime.
  3. Run the list through the instrument on the right so the quarter point is a dollar figure, not a worry. Put September 16 on the calendar with the number attached.
  4. Price planned raises against the 3.2 percent tape, not against January's market. Pay well on purpose, not out of stale fear.
