A 50 percent tariff on Canadian goods lands August 19. Reprice before it reaches your invoices.

What moved

The White House invoked Section 338 of the 1930 Tariff Act for the first time in the statute's history: a 50 percent duty on roughly $20 billion of Canadian goods, cement and wine among them, effective midnight Eastern on August 19. Lumber escapes this one only because it is already taxed elsewhere; Canadian softwood carries about 35 percent in combined duties, and framing lumber is up more than 30 percent since December. The homebuilders put the tariff cost of a new American home at ten thousand dollars.

What it changes

If your inputs touch Canada, cement, aggregates, food and drink, packaging, components, your landed costs step up in nine days, and the quote you gave three weeks ago may already be underwater. This is a repricing problem before it is a sourcing problem: bids need expiry dates, new contracts need a tariff pass-through line, and signed jobs priced on spring materials need a reread before you order against them. Suppliers will fold their own increases into this window, and the invoice will not tell you which line moved.

What not to do

Do not eat a 50 percent input increase to protect a handshake price; reopen the quote and show the customer the tariff line, because they are reading the same headlines you are. Do not stockpile more than you can store and finance to beat one date; this duty is a negotiating position in the USMCA talks and can move again in either direction. And do not take a supplier's word on origin, because the duty follows the country of origin, not the warehouse the truck left.

The tape

Jul 21The proclamations issue: Section 338, dormant since 1930, gets its first use. Fifty percent duties on selected Canadian products, framed openly as pressure in the USMCA renegotiation. Trade lawyers call it an opening bid.
Aug 19The duties take effect at midnight Eastern on roughly $20 billion in goods: cement, wine, and a list built around products the administration ties to Canadian treatment of American cars, alcohol, and dairy.
The stackArticles already covered by Section 232 are exempt, which spares lumber the new 50 but leaves softwood at roughly 35.2 percent combined. Framing lumber is up more than 30 percent from December lows and softwood imports are down 28 percent year over year. The exemption is relief on paper only.
ContextThis lands on top of the new Section 301 baseline of 10 to 12.5 percent announced in late July across dozens of trading partners. The refund story in the previous article is the old regime being unwound; this is the new one being built. Two ledgers, both yours.

Nine days is a repricing window

The businesses that get hurt by tariff moves are rarely the ones paying the duty at the dock; they are the ones holding fixed-price commitments made against costs that no longer exist. A bid issued in July against August 19 materials is a donation. The defensive work is clerical and fast: every open quote gets an expiry date, every new contract gets a pass-through line, and every signed job with affected inputs gets priced for exposure so the decision, absorb, split, or reopen, is made deliberately per job instead of discovered at closeout.

Customers accept repricing they can see the cause of. A line item that says what the tariff did to the inputs converts better than a silent 8 percent bump, and it positions you as the operator who reads the tape rather than the vendor who pads. The one-line clause for everything you issue from today: prices reflect duties in effect at issue; changes in tariff or duty rates flow through at cost, documented.

The play

  1. Pull your top 20 purchased items and mark anything of Canadian origin or from a Canadian supplier. Ask for origin confirmation in writing; the duty follows origin, not the shipping address.
  2. Reprice open quotes and standing bids that carry affected inputs. Add an expiry date and the pass-through clause to everything issued from today.
  3. Run signed jobs through the instrument on the right so each one has an exposure number and a decision: absorb, split, or reopen.
  4. Buy ahead only what storage and cash comfortably allow through September. Hedging a negotiating position with your working capital is a trade you do not need.
