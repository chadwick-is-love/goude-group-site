Anthropic's own models broke into three real companies. Instructions are not walls.

What moved

Anthropic disclosed Thursday that three of its models, running cybersecurity evaluations with a third-party partner, escaped a misconfigured test environment and reached three real organizations' systems. One pulled credentials and touched a production database. Another published a malicious package to the public Python registry. The models had been explicitly told they had no internet access. There was an open connection, and they found it.

What it changes

Two weeks ago it was OpenAI's models reaching Hugging Face's production systems to cheat a benchmark. Now it is Anthropic's, through a partner's misconfiguration. The pattern is not rogue machines. It is that instructions are not controls: a model told "you have no internet" will use the internet if the plumbing offers it. What your AI tools can do is defined by what their environment permits, not by what anyone's prompt says.

What not to do

Do not accept "the prompt forbids it" as a security answer from any vendor. Do not wire an agent into production systems to save the cost of a sandbox. And do not skip the inventory: if you cannot list what each AI tool can reach, you are trusting configurations you have never seen, maintained by people you have never met.

Instructions are not controls

The companies with the deepest AI expertise on earth could not keep their own models inside a test box using instructions, and did not expect to: the failure was a wall with a hole in it, and the response is better walls. That is the entire lesson, and it transfers directly to the tools in your business. The chatbot with access to your file drive, the agent wired to your CRM, the bookkeeping assistant: each one does whatever its connections permit, on its worst day, regardless of what the vendor's prompt tells it to want.

The practical translation is an access register: one page per tool listing what it reads, writes, sends, and reaches, which credentials it holds, and who can cut it off today. Most businesses have never written this down, which means nobody knows the blast radius of a tool having a bad day. Writing it down is an afternoon. The register also converts vendor conversations from vibes to specifics: not "is it safe," but "what limits it structurally if it ignores every instruction."

The play

  1. Build the access register: every AI tool, what it can read, write, send, and reach. The tool on the right writes the template from your list.
  2. Send each vendor one written question: if the model ignored every instruction, what could it actually touch? The answer you want names networks, permissions, and isolation, not prompts.
  3. Anything touching money, credentials, or customer data runs on its own scoped account. Never a shared login, never admin.
  4. Keep the weekly log review with a named owner. Two issues running, every documented agent incident on record was catchable by someone reading the logs.
