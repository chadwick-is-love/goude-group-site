Stolen cards are buying AI credits in ninety dollar bites. Set the alerts today.

What moved

The case that made the rounds this week: a Sussex business owner's Metro Bank card was drained of 14,244 pounds in two days of Claude credit purchases, 90 to 200 pounds at a time. The bank flagged the very first charge, blocked it, then let dozens more through. Korean cardholders hit the same pattern with fraudulent ChatGPT subscriptions in June, and Microsoft has traced phishing campaigns dressed as ChatGPT, Claude, and DeepSeek notices harvesting card numbers at scale.

What it changes

AI credits have become a currency fraud likes: instantly delivered, high limits, and resellable as loaded accounts. To a bank's risk model the charges look clean, since a reputable merchant selling digital goods in mid-size amounts is what normal looks like. And the noise works in the thief's favor: every business now carries a handful of legitimate AI subscriptions, so one more charge from an AI company reads as someone on the team buying credits.

What not to do

Do not assume the bank catches it; Metro flagged charge one and paid out fourteen thousand more. Do not let AI subscriptions ride on cards nobody reconciles, which is where these hide best. And do not shrug off the first small unknown AI charge as a teammate's trial; the Korean pattern started at 19 dollars a head.

The tape

Jun 19The Metro Bank fraud begins: a 90.90 pound Claude credit charge is flagged and confirmed unauthorized. Charges keep landing daily anyway, 90 to 200 pounds each, until the card is finally frozen with 14,244 pounds gone. The bank's explanation cites the complex nature of the fraud; Anthropic ultimately refunds the customer directly. The case surfaced publicly this week.
JuneKorean cardholders report waves of unauthorized ChatGPT Plus and annual subscriptions, 19 to 197 dollars, run through a local payment gateway under merchant names like ChatGPT_NICE. OpenAI confirms stolen card data, disables the payment paths, and completes refunds. Victims could not even access the accounts their cards had paid for.
UpstreamMicrosoft threat intelligence documents phishing campaigns impersonating ChatGPT, Claude, and DeepSeek this spring: thousands of fake subscription and account-violation emails, fake payment pages collecting card numbers, and a poisoned download live within 45 minutes of a model release.
PatternStolen numbers get tested and monetized through AI merchants because the goods are instant and the charges blend in. Reddit and support forums show the same story recurring across banks and borders. This is a category now, not an incident.

Boring controls beat clever fraud

Nothing about the defense is sophisticated, which is the good news. Real-time alerts on every business card, set to a low threshold, turn a two-day drain into a two-charge loss. A written list of the AI services you actually pay for, with the card and the owner next to each, turns "probably someone's subscription" into a thirty-second check. Same-day disputes preserve your chargeback rights, and the bank's own conduct in the Metro case is the argument for speed: the customer who reports at charge two is a different case file than the one who reports at charge forty.

If you take cards online, you are on the other side of the same trade: fraudsters test stolen numbers against small merchants before spending them. Your processor ships defenses for this, velocity limits and checkout challenges among them, and most sit half-configured. A burst of small declines in your processor dashboard is the tell.

The play

  1. Turn on real-time transaction alerts for every business card today, threshold as low as the bank allows. Five minutes per card, and it is the single highest-value control in this story.
  2. Write the approved AI spend list: every AI service you pay for, which card it bills, who owns it. The tool on the right drafts it. Anything billing outside the list gets frozen first and investigated second.
  3. Dispute unknown charges same day, in writing, and freeze the card; do not wait to see whether a pattern develops. The pattern is the loss.
  4. If you accept cards: open your processor's fraud settings this week and enable card-testing protections. Watch for bursts of small declines; that is a test run happening to you.
