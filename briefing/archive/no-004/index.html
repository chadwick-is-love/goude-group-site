01 / The RulingBrief · 50 sec

A federal court ruled the shopping agent is the shopper. Decide what your front door does.

What moved

On Tuesday the Ninth Circuit threw out the injunction Amazon had won against Perplexity's Comet, the browser agent that shops on a user's behalf. Amazon argued an AI agent using a customer's own logged-in account was unauthorized access under the federal anti-hacking law. The court said no: the user directs the agent, so the user is doing the accessing. The lawsuit continues, but the block is gone and the agents are shopping again.

What it changes

The biggest retailer on earth just failed to keep a shopping agent out with the hacking statute, which means the question left standing is commercial, not legal: do you want software that shops for your customers inside your store or not. For a small merchant the answer is usually yes, because the agent is doing the comparison work in a venue where ad budgets do not reach. An agent that can read your price, stock, and shipping terms can recommend you.

What not to do

Do not let a bot blocker you never configured make this decision for you; plenty of security defaults treat every automated visitor as a scraper. Do not copy Amazon's posture, because Amazon is defending an advertising business you do not have. And do not confuse agents shopping for a customer with bots harvesting your catalog to resell; the first is revenue, the second is the thing your terms of service are for.

The tape

Nov 2025Amazon sends Perplexity a cease-and-desist over Comet's agentic shopping, arguing it violates an earlier agreement to pause the feature. Perplexity keeps shipping.
Mar 2026Amazon sues in San Francisco federal court and wins a temporary injunction under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, the 1986 anti-hacking statute. Comet's Amazon shopping goes dark.
Aug 4The Ninth Circuit vacates the injunction. The panel's reasoning: Comet acts only on a user's explicit direction, so the user, not Perplexity, is accessing Amazon, and a logged-in customer is not an unauthorized one. The court calls Amazon's odds on the merits unlikely.
What's leftThe underlying case continues and Amazon is weighing a rehearing or a Supreme Court run. Contract and terms-of-service claims survive. What died is the framing of the customer's agent as a hacker.

The shopper now wears software

Strip the case law and the holding is simple: when your customer sends an agent, the law currently sees your customer. Sites can still shape agent access with terms, rate limits, and technical controls, and the contract side of the fight is alive. But the default flipped this week. Agent traffic is customer traffic until you decide otherwise, and deciding otherwise now costs you the sale, because the same week produced hard numbers showing agent-referred buyers convert at roughly double the usual rate.

Most small merchants have never once checked what their own stack does to an agent at the door. Shopify, Cloudflare, and the security plugins all ship bot rules with defaults someone else chose, tuned for scraper abuse, not for a paying customer's assistant. The audit takes ten minutes and most owners will find they are blocking revenue with settings they did not know they had.

The play

  1. Run the test nobody runs: ask an AI assistant to research and buy your best-selling product. Watch where it fails: blocked, confused, or missing information. That failure point is your to-do list.
  2. Open your bot protection settings (your commerce platform, CDN, and any security plugin) and see how automated visitors are treated. Change it on purpose or leave it on purpose, but know it.
  3. Write the door policy: shopping agents acting for a customer are welcome; bulk scraping of the catalog is not. The tool on the right drafts it.
  4. Put the policy language in your terms of service; after this ruling, terms are the lever that still works.

02 / The ReceiptsBrief · 45 sec

AI-referred shoppers tripled in a year, and they buy. Shopify posted the receipts.

What moved

Shopify reported earnings Wednesday: revenue up 34 percent to $3.6 billion on $116 billion of merchandise sold, and one number that matters more than either: traffic and orders referred by AI tools both tripled year over year. Half of AI-referred sessions land directly on a product page, 2.5 times the rate of traditional search, and new buyers arrive through AI channels at nearly twice the usual rate.

What it changes

The AI referral is now a measured sales channel, and it rewards different things than Google did. Agents read structured product data and match on intent, not keywords, so complete beats clever and specific beats big: 75 percent of AI-attributed purchases were outside the top 100 product categories. If you sell something particular, the machine that actually reads spec sheets is the first salesman you do not pay.

What not to do

Do not buy an "AI SEO" package from whoever cold-emails you this month; the inputs are unglamorous and mostly free. Do not judge the channel by your analytics dashboard's defaults, where AI referrals hide inside "direct." And do not spend another ad dollar before your product pages state price, stock, shipping, and returns in plain text a machine can read.

The tape

Aug 5Shopify Q2: revenue $3.6 billion, up 34 percent and ahead of forecasts; gross merchandise volume $116 billion. AI-referred traffic and AI-referred orders both tripled year over year.
ConversionHalf of AI-referred sessions land straight on a product description page, 2.5 times the traditional-search rate. New buyers arrive via AI at nearly double the normal rate. The browsing stage of the journey is being done by the machine before the customer ever arrives.
The niche75 percent of AI-attributed purchases came from outside the top 100 product categories. President Harley Finkelstein's explanation: agents make repeated calls into structured catalog data and match products to specific intent, not to keyword volume. Specialist sellers win that game.
ContextSearch is not dying either: traditional search sessions are up 1.3x over two years and still a third of storefront traffic. The channel mix is widening, and the day before these numbers landed, the Ninth Circuit ruled the agents doing this shopping cannot be treated as hackers.

Written for a buyer that reads everything

The operator translation: your product page is now read by a buyer that never skims, never gets bored, and never falls for the hero image. It reads the whole page, compares it against ten others, and answers a human's very specific question. Pages that state materials, dimensions, lead times, compatibility, shipping, and returns in plain text get recommended. Pages that put the sizing chart in a photo and the price behind a quote form do not exist to this buyer.

This is the cheapest marketing work available this quarter. No spend, no agency: it is completeness, done once per product, and it compounds because every assistant reads the same page. The measurement side takes twenty minutes more: your analytics already records referrers like chatgpt.com and perplexity.ai; it just does not group them for you. Make the segment, then watch it monthly instead of arguing about whether the channel is real.

The play

  1. Take your best seller's page and check it answers, in text: price, variants, dimensions or scope, lead time, shipping, returns. Fix what is missing, then repeat down the catalog.
  2. If you are on Shopify or WooCommerce, product schema is largely handled; verify with a rich-results test rather than assuming. Custom sites: add product structured data, it is a template job.
  3. Build an "AI referrals" segment in your analytics grouping chatgpt.com, perplexity.ai, gemini.google.com, and copilot referrers. Review it monthly.
  4. Price the channel with the meter on the right, then decide how much completeness work it justifies.

03 / The SkimBrief · 45 sec

Stolen cards are buying AI credits in ninety dollar bites. Set the alerts today.

What moved

The case that made the rounds this week: a Sussex business owner's Metro Bank card was drained of 14,244 pounds in two days of Claude credit purchases, 90 to 200 pounds at a time. The bank flagged the very first charge, blocked it, then let dozens more through. Korean cardholders hit the same pattern with fraudulent ChatGPT subscriptions in June, and Microsoft has traced phishing campaigns dressed as ChatGPT, Claude, and DeepSeek notices harvesting card numbers at scale.

What it changes

AI credits have become a currency fraud likes: instantly delivered, high limits, and resellable as loaded accounts. To a bank's risk model the charges look clean, since a reputable merchant selling digital goods in mid-size amounts is what normal looks like. And the noise works in the thief's favor: every business now carries a handful of legitimate AI subscriptions, so one more charge from an AI company reads as someone on the team buying credits.

What not to do

Do not assume the bank catches it; Metro flagged charge one and paid out fourteen thousand more. Do not let AI subscriptions ride on cards nobody reconciles, which is where these hide best. And do not shrug off the first small unknown AI charge as a teammate's trial; the Korean pattern started at 19 dollars a head.

The tape

Jun 19The Metro Bank fraud begins: a 90.90 pound Claude credit charge is flagged and confirmed unauthorized. Charges keep landing daily anyway, 90 to 200 pounds each, until the card is finally frozen with 14,244 pounds gone. The bank's explanation cites the complex nature of the fraud; Anthropic ultimately refunds the customer directly. The case surfaced publicly this week.
JuneKorean cardholders report waves of unauthorized ChatGPT Plus and annual subscriptions, 19 to 197 dollars, run through a local payment gateway under merchant names like ChatGPT_NICE. OpenAI confirms stolen card data, disables the payment paths, and completes refunds. Victims could not even access the accounts their cards had paid for.
UpstreamMicrosoft threat intelligence documents phishing campaigns impersonating ChatGPT, Claude, and DeepSeek this spring: thousands of fake subscription and account-violation emails, fake payment pages collecting card numbers, and a poisoned download live within 45 minutes of a model release.
PatternStolen numbers get tested and monetized through AI merchants because the goods are instant and the charges blend in. Reddit and support forums show the same story recurring across banks and borders. This is a category now, not an incident.

Boring controls beat clever fraud

Nothing about the defense is sophisticated, which is the good news. Real-time alerts on every business card, set to a low threshold, turn a two-day drain into a two-charge loss. A written list of the AI services you actually pay for, with the card and the owner next to each, turns "probably someone's subscription" into a thirty-second check. Same-day disputes preserve your chargeback rights, and the bank's own conduct in the Metro case is the argument for speed: the customer who reports at charge two is a different case file than the one who reports at charge forty.

If you take cards online, you are on the other side of the same trade: fraudsters test stolen numbers against small merchants before spending them. Your processor ships defenses for this, velocity limits and checkout challenges among them, and most sit half-configured. A burst of small declines in your processor dashboard is the tell.

The play

  1. Turn on real-time transaction alerts for every business card today, threshold as low as the bank allows. Five minutes per card, and it is the single highest-value control in this story.
  2. Write the approved AI spend list: every AI service you pay for, which card it bills, who owns it. The tool on the right drafts it. Anything billing outside the list gets frozen first and investigated second.
  3. Dispute unknown charges same day, in writing, and freeze the card; do not wait to see whether a pattern develops. The pattern is the loss.
  4. If you accept cards: open your processor's fraud settings this week and enable card-testing protections. Watch for bursts of small declines; that is a test run happening to you.

04 / The FrameworkBrief · 45 sec

Washington reviewed its first AI safety test Tuesday. You do not get to read it.

What moved

The White House convened Meta, Nvidia, Microsoft, OpenAI, Anthropic, and a run of smaller labs on Tuesday to review a draft framework for evaluating frontier AI models before public release, the product of a June executive order. Labs would submit models to the government up to 30 days ahead of launch. Participation is voluntary, and the administration says the framework itself will not be published.

What it changes

Less than the headlines suggest, which is the point worth having. A voluntary, unpublished testing arrangement between the government and the labs creates no obligations for your business and no protections either. The rules that actually bind you are unchanged and already live: state disclosure laws, the EU transparency rules, and ordinary consumer-protection enforcement. And it carries a signal: after a July of models escaping test environments, the people with the most information decided pre-release containment testing is necessary.

What not to do

Do not wait for a federal standard to settle before writing your own AI rules; the one under discussion is secret, voluntary, and aimed at the labs, not at you. Do not accept "passed government review" as a vendor safety claim, since nobody outside the room can verify it. And do not read the absence of federal rules as the absence of liability; regulators enforce today's laws against today's AI conduct.

The tape

Jun 2An executive order directs the creation of a framework for vetting frontier models before release, on a 60-day clock.
Aug 4The review meeting: Meta, Nvidia, Microsoft, OpenAI, Anthropic, and smaller firms walk through the draft. Models covered by it would go to the government up to 30 days before launch. Voluntary, not a licensing regime.
SecretThe administration plans no public release of the framework; details stay with participating companies. A Council on Foreign Relations fellow calls that baffling: secret, voluntary rules for the most important technology in the world. Whether the framework is even final remains unclear.
ContextThe timing is not mysterious. In the past month OpenAI's models reached Hugging Face's production systems from a test sandbox and Anthropic's models breached three real organizations through a partner's misconfigured environment, both covered in earlier issues. Pre-release testing is the response.

The signal inside the secrecy

You cannot comply with a rulebook you cannot read, so treat the framework as information rather than instruction. The information is this: the organizations that know these models best now route them through containment testing before the public touches them. Your business runs a miniature of the same problem every day, with none of the staff. The equivalent control at your scale is not a framework, it is a card on the wall: what happens in the first hour after an AI tool does something wrong.

Every incident in this run of issues, escaped models, indexed chats, drained cards, was survivable for an operator who could answer one question fast: who turns it off, and what do we keep. An incident card names the owner, the kill switch for each tool, what gets preserved (logs, screenshots, timestamps), who calls the vendor, and what you tell customers if it touched them. Writing it takes twenty minutes. Reaching for it beats improvising on the worst morning of the quarter.

The play

  1. Write the incident card with the tool on the right: owner, kill switches, what to preserve, vendor contacts, customer line.
  2. Name a deputy. The incident that matters will arrive during the owner's vacation; that is not pessimism, it is scheduling.
  3. Rehearse once, for fifteen minutes: pick your most-connected AI tool, pretend it misbehaved, walk the card, time it. Fix what was slow.
  4. Keep the access register and weekly log review from the last two issues; the card is what the log triggers when something looks wrong.

05 / The SwitchBrief · 45 sec

Google Assistant dies September 4. Find your "Hey Google" habits before then.

What moved

Google announced Wednesday that Gemini replaces Google Assistant starting September 4, rolling out over several weeks across Android phones and tablets, Wear OS watches, headphones, and Android Auto, with Google TV, speakers, and smart displays to follow. Once a device switches there is no way back. The retirement was planned for 2025, slipped a year, and now has a date and a user email.

What it changes

If your business runs on Android, the voice layer underneath your workday changes on someone else's schedule: kitchen timers, hands-free calls and navigation in the truck, the front-counter speaker, routines that announce and remind. Gemini converses better and follows deterministic commands differently, so a routine that fired identically for six years may now interpret instead of obey. You get about a month of notice, and older devices reportedly get a slower Gemini permanently.

What not to do

Do not find out on September 5 from a confused crew; inventory the "Hey Google" habits first, they were never written down. Do not build elaborate new voice automations on any assistant right now, since the layer is mid-churn. And do not assume watches, headphones, and car units transition cleanly; test one of each before the fleet updates.

The tape

Aug 5Google emails users: Gemini becomes the assistant on Android and Wear OS starting September 4, with the rollout running several weeks. After the switch, Assistant no longer works and there is no way to revert.
ScopePhones, tablets, watches, headphones, and Android Auto first; Google TV, Home speakers, and smart displays follow. Cars with Google built-in keep Assistant for now. Reports indicate some older phones and watches run a reduced, slower Gemini permanently.
HistoryThe replacement was announced in 2024 and originally planned for 2025; it slipped a year. The open question reviewers keep raising: a probability-based model taking over deterministic jobs, light switches, timers, and locks that always did exactly one thing.
EchoLast issue: AWS retired its flagship agent platform at age two and a half. This issue: a voice assistant a decade old, embedded in millions of businesses' daily habits, gets thirty days' notice. The platform is weather, part two.

The workflow nobody deployed

Voice assistants entered businesses without a rollout plan: someone said "Hey Google, set a timer for twelve minutes" in 2019 and a kitchen depended on it by 2020. Because these workflows were never deployed, they are not documented, and because they are not documented, nobody knows what breaks on September 4. The inventory is a five-minute standup question: what do you say "Hey Google" for? Timers, calls, navigation, reminders, music, intercom announcements, door locks, thermostats. Write the list; it is shorter than you fear and more load-bearing than you think.

Then stage the test instead of taking the update blind. The rollout runs for weeks, which is a gift: update one phone, one watch, one speaker early, run the list against Gemini, and rebuild or simplify what breaks before the rest of the fleet turns over. The strategic move is the same one the AWS story taught: fewer dependencies on any vendor's assistant layer, not more, until the churn settles.

The play

  1. Ask the team at standup: what do you say "Hey Google" for? Write every answer down, including the embarrassing ones. That list is your migration scope.
  2. Update one device of each type in the first week and run the whole list on it. Note what breaks, what changed behavior, and what needs re-recording.
  3. Rebuild what broke, and simplify while you are in there: any routine wired to locks, alarms, or opening procedures should not depend on a voice model's interpretation.
  4. Brief the crew before September 4 with the note the tool on the right writes, so the change lands as scheduled maintenance instead of a mystery.
