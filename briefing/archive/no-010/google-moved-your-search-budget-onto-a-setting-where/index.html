The Briefing · No. 010 · September 21, 2026 · The Migration
05 / The MigrationBrief · 45 sec

Google moved your search budget onto a setting where its AI chooses the queries. The one grading how that went is Google.

What moved

Since September 1, Google has been converting campaigns that use campaign-level broad match or automatically created assets into AI Max, aiming to finish by the end of September. Search term matching switches on by default, and for automatically created assets campaigns, so does AI-written ad text. Google cites 7 percent more conversions at similar cost. An independent study of more than 250 campaigns found cost per acquisition up a median 16 percent.

What it changes

Your ads are now showing on searches you did not choose, and the report that tells you whether that worked comes from the company selling the clicks. At $50,000 a month in search spend, a 16 percent rise in acquisition cost is roughly $96,000 a year for the same customers. The switch happened in your account whether or not anyone at your agency mentioned it.

What not to do

Do not assume your agency opted you out; the migration runs in place and the defaults favor more matching. Do not judge AI Max on Google's conversion column alone; compare cost per acquisition against your own CRM for the same weeks. Do not let AI-written ad text run on regulated claims or pricing without someone approving it.

The tape

Aug 3Google stops allowing new campaign-level broad match or automatically created assets setups across the interface, Google Ads Editor and the API.
Aug 5Affected advertisers receive Google's email notice of the automatic conversion to AI Max.
Sep 1Migration begins and runs progressively through September, with search term matching on by default and brand lists kept.
Sep 4Google confirms a small test letting exact and phrase match keywords show in AI Mode for explicit, direct intent, a second route into AI surfaces.
Sep 30Google's target to finish eligible conversions. Dynamic Search Ads follow in February 2027.

The meter and the referee are the same company

The mechanism is simple. Search term matching lets Google's model decide which queries are close enough to your keywords to buy. More matching means more impressions and more spend, and the headline 7 percent lift is Google's own measurement on its own data. Independent readings disagree: higher conversion value in some retail accounts, but at a higher cost per acquisition, and one study found invalid traffic doubling on AI Max over nine months. None of that means turn it off. It means someone other than Google has to check.

Ask your agency for a list of every campaign that was converted and the date it happened. Pull cost per acquisition from your own CRM for the four weeks before and after. Where cost rose and qualified leads did not, turn search term matching off for that campaign, or ring-fence it with brand and URL exclusions. Keep it where it earned its place. The decision should rest on numbers you own.

The play

  1. Your marketing lead asks the agency this week for every campaign converted to AI Max, with the date and which features are now on.
  2. Your controller pulls cost per acquisition from the CRM for four weeks either side of each conversion date.
  3. Put a yearly dollar figure on the cost drift at your spend with the free tool, The Drift.
  4. Where acquisition cost rose without more qualified leads, the agency turns search term matching off or adds exclusions, and reports back in two weeks.
