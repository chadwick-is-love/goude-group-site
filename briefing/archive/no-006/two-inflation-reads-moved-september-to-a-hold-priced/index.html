Two inflation reads moved September to a hold, priced near two to one. Test any fixed-rate offer against that before you sign it.

What moved

Wednesday's consumer price report put July inflation at 3.4 percent with core at 2.5, and futures moved to about a 64 percent chance the Fed holds at 3.50 to 3.75 percent on September 16, up from 52 the day before. Thursday's producer report ran hot underneath, core wholesale up 0.4 in the month and 4.7 on the year, which argues against cuts even as the hike fades.

What it changes

No. 005 covered the jobs report knocking the September increase off base case; this week's consumer read kept it off, and that is the update. Every floating-rate instrument you carry, credit line, SBA loan, equipment note, prices off that meeting. Lenders sell fixed-rate conversions hardest when owners are nervous, and the premium on those offers is exactly what the lock test measures: how many hikes must land before the safety you bought pays for itself.

What not to do

Do not sign a fixed conversion because the pitch mentions uncertainty; price it first. Do not treat a hold as a cut; nothing this week made money cheaper, it made more expensive money less likely, which is a different fact. And do not let September 16 arrive without your exposure written down: if the decision surprises in either direction, your floating balances reprice within the billing cycle, not next quarter.

What the lock actually costs

A fixed-rate conversion is insurance, and insurance has a price: the spread between the fixed rate offered and the floating rate you pay today. When hike odds fade, the risk being insured gets smaller while the premium usually does not move, which quietly makes the same offer a worse deal than it was in July. The wholesale side is why this is a hold-versus-hike argument rather than a cut story: producers' core prices rose 0.4 percent in one month, and a central bank staring at 4.7 percent wholesale inflation does not ease into it. Both directions are priced weekly; your lender's pitch deck is not.

So run the arithmetic before the meeting rather than after. Get the fixed offer in writing, with rate and every fee, and count the quarter-point hikes required before the lock beats floating; the tool does it in seconds. If the answer is two or more inside two years, floating with a written trigger plan usually wins: a one-page note saying what you refinance, cut, or reprice the afternoon a hike actually lands. The trade-off is sleep. Some owners pay the spread happily to stop watching the Fed, and that is a legitimate purchase; the mistake is not buying insurance, it is buying it unpriced.

The play

  1. Put every floating balance on one sheet: lender, balance, rate, and what a quarter point adds. That page is your September 16 exposure, and it takes twenty minutes.
  2. Get any fixed-rate conversion offer in writing with rate and fees, then run it through the tool on the right before you respond to the pitch.
  3. If break-even needs two or more hikes, keep floating and write the trigger plan: the specific action you take the afternoon an increase actually lands.
  4. Recheck the odds the morning of September 16, not the week a lender calls; the number moves weekly and the pitch does not.
