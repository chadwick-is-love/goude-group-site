01 / Rates and LendingBrief · 45 sec

The Fed's next move is a hike. Most owners are still waiting for a cut.

What moved

The Fed meets Tuesday and Wednesday. Markets price roughly two-in-three odds of a hold this week and one-in-three of a quarter-point hike, with September priced far more aggressively. June's projections showed half the committee expecting at least one hike this year, up from none in March. June CPI actually fell on the month, but five straight years above target keep the direction pointed up.

What it changes

Every dollar of variable-rate debt: SBA 7(a) loans, credit lines, floating commercial mortgages, all pegged to prime, all repricing upward if the cycle turns. Fixed rates quoted today are cheaper than the same paper after the first hike. Any plan that starts with "once rates come down" is a plan built on the wrong direction.

What not to do

Do not float a renewal or balloon into the fall assuming rates soften. Do not draw on a prime-plus line without pricing prime's path. Do not confuse a hold this week with the trend: half the committee wants a hike this year.

The tape

Jun 17The Fed holds. The statement is stripped down, Chair Warsh declines forward guidance, and the dot plot shows 9 of 18 policymakers expecting at least one hike by year-end. In March, that number was zero.
Mid-JulJune CPI falls 0.4% on the month, the first monthly decline since 2020. PPI drops 0.3%. Cooling data is the entire case for a hold.
Jul 25Futures markets price about 65% odds of a hold this week, 35% of a quarter-point hike, and roughly 80% odds of a hike by September.
Jul 28The meeting starts. The statement lands Wednesday afternoon.

What this actually means

The debate has quietly flipped. For two years the question was when cuts arrive. The question now is whether the first hike lands in September or later, and lenders reprice sentiment before the Fed moves a basis point. Term sheets tighten, lock windows shorten, and the fixed-rate quote you got in June ages fast.

The math is unglamorous and worth doing. A quarter point on $500,000 of floating debt is about $1,250 a year. Three hikes is $3,750, every year, for doing nothing. Small numbers alone, meaningful stacked against a margin that tariffs and wages are already squeezing. The move is not fear. It is knowing your number and deciding on purpose what stays floating.

The play

  1. Inventory every variable-rate dollar: lines, SBA loans, floating mortgages, equipment notes. Write down the index and margin on each.
  2. Price the hike with the tool on the right. If one quarter point hurts, three will.
  3. Ask your lender for a fixed-rate conversion quote this week, before Wednesday's statement moves the window.
  4. If a renewal or balloon lands before year-end, start the refinance now. Term sheets stall once a hiking cycle starts.

02 / Tariff MoneyBrief · 45 sec

Washington is refunding $166 billion in tariffs. Half is still unclaimed.

What moved

The Supreme Court voided the IEEPA tariffs in February. As of mid-July, Customs has returned $86.3 billion of roughly $166 billion owed, $49.1 billion of it in June alone. Phase 3 of the refund system, covering finally liquidated entries worth about $11.4 billion, opens at the end of this month. Refunds carry statutory interest of 6 to 7 percent, compounded daily from the day you paid.

What it changes

If you imported goods between April 2025 and February 2026 and paid IEEPA duties, you are owed principal plus interest. For many small importers that is five figures or more of working capital sitting in a federal queue. The refund is real; the failure mode is administrative. Right now 8,384 approved claims are stuck on bad banking details alone.

What not to do

Do not assume your customs broker filed for you. Do not wait out the government's appeal: the refund order stands and the money is moving now. Do not let a rejected declaration sit in the queue. Refile it.

The tape

Feb 20The Supreme Court strikes down the IEEPA tariffs. The Court of International Trade orders Customs to give the money back.
Apr 20CBP opens CAPE, the refund filing system. Clean claims pay out in 60 to 90 days.
JuneThe biggest month yet: $49.1 billion refunded, more than double the new tariff revenue collected in the same month.
Jul 10$86.3 billion returned, roughly $80 billion still outstanding. Phase 3, for finally liquidated entries, confirmed on track for end of July. The Federal Circuit appeal continues in parallel.

The catch is administrative, not legal

The legal question is settled enough that checks are flowing. What stops a refund now is paperwork: a declaration never filed, an entry flagged for antidumping duties, or banking details in ACE that do not match, which alone has 8,384 approved claims parked. The government's appeal contests parts of the process, including Phase 3 eligibility, but it has not stopped disbursement.

The importer of record gets the money. If a broker or freight forwarder was your importer of record, the refund routes through them, and your claim on it is a commercial conversation you want to start in writing, this week, not after they receive it.

The play

  1. Pull your import records for April 2025 through February 2026: entry summaries, duties paid, and who the importer of record was.
  2. Ask your broker one question in writing: has a CAPE declaration been filed for every entry, and what does the REV-615 status report show.
  3. Verify your ACH banking details in ACE before the money tries to land.
  4. If any entries were finally liquidated, calendar the Phase 3 opening and file the day it opens.

03 / InsuranceBrief · 40 sec

Insurance got cheaper everywhere except where you get sued.

What moved

Marsh's second-quarter index landed Thursday: global commercial rates fell 6 percent, the eighth straight quarterly decline. Property fell 12 percent, cyber 4 percent for its twelfth straight drop, financial lines 3 percent. The exception is US casualty: up 7 percent overall, up 11 percent excluding workers' comp. Carriers are also offering broader coverage, higher limits, and lower retentions to keep accounts.

What it changes

Your renewal is two negotiations, not one. Property, cyber, and management lines should come back cheaper with better terms, and in a falling market a flat renewal is a concession, not a win. Liability and commercial auto will come in higher, and need structure work, not just budget: retentions, limits, and the risk file your underwriter reads.

What not to do

Do not accept a single package number: the falling lines will quietly subsidize the rising one inside the bundle. Do not skip remarketing out of loyalty to the incumbent. Do not buy less liability limit to save premium in the one line where verdicts keep climbing.

The quarter, by line

GlobalComposite rates down 6 percent in Q2, after 5 percent in Q1. Eight consecutive quarters of decline.
PropertyDown 12 percent globally, down 13 percent in the US. The steepest discounts in the market.
CyberDown 4 percent, the twelfth consecutive quarterly decrease. Broader terms widely available.
CasualtyThe outlier. US casualty up 7 percent, up 11 percent excluding workers' comp. The only major line rising anywhere.

Soft market rules

In a falling market, capacity chases your premium. Marsh reports carriers offering broader coverage, higher limits, and reduced retentions to hold accounts, which means price is only the first thing to negotiate. The window stays open until the next major catastrophe closes it, so the discount belongs to whoever remarkets at this renewal, not eventually.

Casualty runs on different physics: jury verdicts and litigation costs are still climbing, and no amount of shopping changes that. What you control is structure and story. A higher retention you can actually carry buys down the increase. A clean risk file, signed contracts with indemnification, a written driver policy, corrected claims history, is the difference between the 7 percent increase and the 15 the underwriter opens with.

The play

  1. Start 90 days before renewal and instruct your broker, in writing, to remarket every line. The tool on the right writes that instruction.
  2. Demand line-by-line pricing. Never accept one package number.
  3. On property and cyber, push past price: broader coverage, higher limits, and lower retentions are on the table this quarter.
  4. On liability and auto, ask what retention level buys the increase down, and tighten the file the underwriter sees: contracts, driver policy, claims corrections.

04 / LaborBrief · 45 sec

Layoffs just hit a 57-year low. Hiring froze with them.

What moved

Initial jobless claims fell to 187,000 last week, the lowest reading since 1969, down 22,000 in a single week. Under the headline, the market has split: senior-level job postings are up 15 percent year over year while entry-level postings are down 6.3 percent. Economists are calling it low-hire, low-fire. Nobody lets go, nobody takes on.

What it changes

Replacement, not recruiting, is your labor risk now. Nobody is firing people like your best people, so the like-for-like replacement pool is not refilling, and demand for senior operators is climbing anyway. Meanwhile the entry level is a buyer's market: experienced candidates are competing down into junior roles.

What not to do

Do not run retention by counteroffer: by resignation day the decision is made, and the recruiter who found them once will call again. Do not read record-low layoffs as an easy hiring market: the pipeline is frozen. Do not staff a senior gap with a junior hire and hope.

What the number hides

The 187,000 print overstates the calm. Fewer laid-off workers file at all: eligibility has tightened to the point that fewer than a third of unemployed people qualify for benefits, so claims measure a shrinking slice of the churn. What the number does say clearly is that employers are holding on to who they have, everywhere, at once.

The split underneath is the operational fact. Postings for senior roles are up 15 percent on the year while entry-level postings fell 6.3 percent, and long-tenured jobseekers report shrinking negotiating power. Stable for incumbents, brutal for entrants, and tight at exactly the level where your business is hardest to backfill.

The operator's read

Your labor risk concentrates in two or three named people, so price it. Replacing a senior operator runs one to one-and-a-half times salary once you count the search, the vacancy months, and the ramp, and in this market the vacancy months stretch. The raise that keeps that person costs a fraction of the search that replaces them, and it only works before the recruiter calls.

The other side of the freeze is leverage: the entry level is the best buyer's market in years. The roles you deferred filling are now fielding stronger candidates than the market you remember. Defend the top of your roster and upgrade the bottom.

The play

  1. Name the three people whose exit would hurt most. Run the tool on the right for each one.
  2. Move compensation on your calendar, not theirs: a raise now beats a counteroffer later, and beats a search every time.
  3. Put in writing what each key person's next two years here look like. Ambiguity is what recruiters sell against.
  4. Use the frozen bottom: post the entry-level roles you postponed. The candidates got better while you waited.

05 / The StackBrief · 45 sec

Your payment processor is in play. Your software bill is a test.

What moved

PayPal's board rejected a $53.4 billion all-cash bid from Stripe and Advent on July 20, holding out for roughly $70 a share against the $60.50 offered. The bidders want a deal by month-end; PayPal reports earnings Tuesday. A combined company would touch about $3.7 trillion in annual payment volume. Separately, Squarespace raised subscription prices as much as 26 percent, then reversed within a week, calling it an internal error.

What it changes

The rails and tools you run the business on are consolidating and testing price. Fewer independent processors means less pricing pressure on your rates later: whatever leverage your volume carries is worth more now than after any ink dries. And the Squarespace week proved the other point: vendor increases are softer than they look. Public pushback reversed a 26 percent hike in seven days.

What not to do

Do not migrate platforms on deal headlines: for merchants, nothing changes for quarters, if ever, and an antitrust review would run long. Do not let an annual plan auto-renew without reading the new price against the old one. Do not accept "the rate is the rate" from a processor you bring real volume to.

The tape

Jul 15Stripe and Advent bid about $53.4 billion, $60.50 a share in cash, for PayPal. The stock jumps.
Jul 17Squarespace price increases of up to 26 percent start landing in customer inboxes. The backlash is immediate and public.
Jul 20PayPal's board formally rejects: the premium is 28 percent, the board wants about $70, and a combined entity would process roughly $3.7 trillion a year, well over half of global online payment volume, an antitrust review in waiting.
Jul 24Squarespace walks the increase back as an "internal error." Analysts had already described it as an across-tier repricing.
Jul 28PayPal earnings. The bidders want an answer by month-end, so the quarter's numbers become the negotiation.

Why a deal between giants is your line item

You do not pick the winner in a consolidation. You pay the spread after it. Two independent giants competing for your checkout is what holds processing rates near cost; one owner of both is what lets them drift. Whether this deal closes or not, the direction of the industry is fewer counterparties, which makes this the right season to learn the one number most owners cannot quote: the effective rate, all processing fees divided by card volume. Everyone knows their volume. Almost nobody knows their rate.

The software bill works the same way at smaller scale. Per-seat creep, tier migrations, legacy-plan retirements: each one is a test of what you will absorb quietly. Squarespace priced to silence and got noise instead, and the reversal took a week. The lesson is not that vendors are villains. It is that these prices are elastic, and silence is what makes them stick.

The play

  1. Pull one month's processing statement and compute your effective rate with the tool on the right. Fees divided by volume. That number is your negotiating position.
  2. Put your annual card volume in writing and ask your processor and one competitor for their best all-in price against it.
  3. Calendar every annual software renewal 30 days ahead. Read the renewal price, not the welcome price.
  4. When a vendor tests a price on you, answer in writing and in public. The market just showed you it works.
