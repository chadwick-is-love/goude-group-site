01 / Works nowBrief · 45 sec

Four in ten small businesses say AI raised their revenue.

What moved

On May 12 Intuit published its 2026 AI Impact Report, built from more than 34,000 small and midsize business owner responses and data from 5.3 million QuickBooks businesses. It is Intuit's own survey. In the U.S., 77% of businesses use AI regularly, up from 48% in July 2024. 78% say it improved productivity and 43% say it raised revenue. Only 2% say revenue fell. Businesses reporting more hiring from AI outnumber those reporting less 4 to 1.

What it changes

Read it as a vendor's number, from a company that sells small business software. Even so, the money question has an answer: 86% of businesses that paid for AI in 2024 were still paying in 2025, and only about 12% pay for dedicated AI tools at all. Most who pay, keep paying. Before you add a seat, decide what hour of your team's time it has to return each month.

What not to do

Do not buy seats for the whole team because 43% said revenue rose; that is a self-reported figure. Do not treat 'productivity improved' as a result until you count hours. Do not pay for a tool nobody on the team can name a weekly job for. Do not skip the count: log the hours for 30 days, before and after. Do not let a tool renew on autopilot.

The tape

Jul 2024Intuit's baseline: 48% of U.S. small and midsize businesses report using AI regularly, and 46% say it improved productivity. Those are the starting points its 2026 report measures against.
202586% of businesses that paid for AI tools in 2024 were still paying in 2025, per Intuit's QuickBooks data. Roughly one in ten businesses in every market pays for dedicated AI tools.
May 12Intuit publishes its 2026 AI Impact Report: 34,000 owner responses plus data from 5.3 million QuickBooks businesses. 77% use AI regularly, 78% report better productivity, 43% report higher revenue and 2% lower revenue.
Oct 26Intuit's fall QuickBooks release adds AI invoice drafts, AI reconciliation and connectors to ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini. Its survey line says 3 in 4 small businesses using AI report productivity gains.
NextBefore your next renewal, count hours on one task for 30 days. Intuit's survey is the vendor's view. Your own timesheet is the evidence that decides whether the seat stays.

A paid seat needs a weekly job

The survey's best number is not the 43%. It is the 86%. Owners who paid for AI in 2024 mostly kept paying in 2025, and keeping a subscription usually means a task the tool does every week. The 43% is a feeling, asked in a survey run by a company that sells accounting software. Only 12% of businesses paid for dedicated AI tools between 2021 and 2025, so paying is still the exception. The question to answer is narrow: which task, how many hours, at what cost per hour.

Do the math before you buy. Take the monthly price of one seat and divide it by your loaded hourly cost for the person using it. A $30 seat for someone who costs you $40 an hour has to return 45 minutes a month. That is a low bar, and a weekly task like first drafts or quote follow-ups clears it. The trade-off is checking time. If reviewing the output eats the minutes it saves, the seat is a cost. Start with one person, one task, and a written hour count.

The play

  1. You, this week: list every AI subscription on the company card, who uses it and what weekly job it does. Cancel any with no named job.
  2. Pick one paid seat and one repeating task, such as quote follow-ups or the Monday report. Time the task by hand for one week and write down the minutes.
  3. Paste into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini: "Here is a task my team does every week: [describe it]. List the steps, mark which ones you can draft, and tell me what a person must check before it goes out."
  4. Run the same task with AI for two weeks. Log minutes saved and minutes spent checking.
  5. Use The Break-Even Hour on this page to see how many minutes a month a seat has to return before it pays for itself at your hourly cost.
  6. At day 30, keep, drop or downgrade the seat on the log, not on the survey.

02 / BewareBrief · 45 sec

Four AI note-taking tools are being sued over recording people without consent.

What moved

Lawsuits now name Otter.ai, Fireflies.ai, Granola and Microsoft Teams live transcription. In re Otter.AI Privacy Litigation, in federal court in California, survived a motion to dismiss on August 13: wiretap, California privacy and biometric voice claims proceed. Fireflies faces two Illinois biometric suits. Granola was sued July 30. A Teams transcription suit was filed February 5. All of these are allegations. No court has found liability.

What it changes

The vendors are the defendants, but the bot sits on your calls. The Otter plaintiffs allege it joins a meeting when the host has connected Zoom, Teams or Google Meet to their account, even when other participants never signed up. One summary lists California's privacy law at $5,000 per violation with no proof of harm required. It lists 11 all-party consent states, including California, Florida and Illinois.

What not to do

Do not let an AI notetaker join a call until everyone on it has been told. Do not leave auto-join switched on in anyone's calendar. Do not record a call with a customer in California, Illinois or another all-party state without a clear yes. Do not keep transcripts forever; set a deletion date. Do not leave vendor AI training on by default. Ask your lawyer about your own states.

The tape

Dec 18Cruz v. Fireflies.AI Corp. is filed in federal court in central Illinois under the state's biometric privacy law. One complaint alleges Fireflies captures the voices of meeting participants who are not its users. A second Illinois suit follows in March.
Feb 5A biometric privacy suit over Microsoft Teams live transcription is filed in federal court in Western Washington. The defendant is Microsoft, whose Teams is already inside many small business subscriptions. A motion to dismiss is pending.
Jul 30Chamberlain v. Granola is filed in federal court in northern California as a proposed class action. It claims violations of California's privacy act and the federal Electronic Communications Privacy Act. It is pending.
Aug 13The judge in the consolidated Otter case partly denies the motion to dismiss. Federal wiretap, California privacy and biometric voice claims proceed. Computer-intrusion counts are dismissed. Mealey's reports the ruling on August 26.
NextThe sources list no trial dates. Before your next client call, check which notetaker bots your team's calendars let in, and who gets told that the call is recorded.

The bot joins before anyone agrees

Most owners think consent is the host's call. The Otter plaintiffs argue otherwise: the bot can join, record and transcribe participants who never created an account and never saw a notice. That is why the cases use wiretap statutes, California's privacy law and Illinois's biometric voice law at once. The judge let those claims proceed in August, while dismissing the computer-intrusion counts. Nothing is decided. The rulings so far only say the claims can continue. Still, a lawsuit costs money even when a vendor wins, and the vendor is not the one sitting in the meeting with your customer.

The fix is a notice habit, not a ban. Put one line in the invite saying the call is recorded and transcribed by an AI tool. Say it again out loud at the start and drop it in chat. If anyone objects, stop the bot and take notes by hand. Turn off speaker identification when anyone is in Illinois, since the biometric suits turn on voiceprints. Pick a retention window and switch off vendor AI training. The trade-off is a slightly slower first minute of every call. That costs less than defending a class claim.

The play

  1. You, today: ask each person who runs calls which notetaker they use: Otter, Fireflies, Granola, Teams transcription or Zoom's. Write the list down.
  2. In each tool's settings, switch off auto-join to calendar events and turn off use of your recordings for AI training. Screenshot the setting.
  3. Paste into ChatGPT or Claude: "Here is our current meeting notetaker policy: [paste, or say we have none]. Our customers are in these states: [list]. Tell me which meetings need a verbal notice and which need a written yes, and draft a two-sentence notice for each."
  4. Add one sentence to every calendar invite template: the call is recorded and transcribed by an AI tool, and anyone can ask us to turn it off.
  5. Use The Recording Notice on this page to write your one-page rule, the opening script and the retention window.
  6. Call your business lawyer or broker once. Ask whether your policies cover privacy claims from recording, and which states they want you to treat as all-party.
Sources

BuildBetter: AI notetaker cases, courts, statutes and penalties as of October 15, a vendor blog · Mealey's: Otter.ai class action ruling, claims dismissed and kept, August 26

The suits allege the bot joins whatever call its host connects, so guests who never signed up get recorded. California's law pays $5,000 a violation with no proof of harm.

03 / PriceBrief · 45 sec

Microsoft's $18 Copilot price for small business ends December 31.

What moved

Microsoft's pricing page lists Microsoft 365 Copilot Business at $18 a user a month on an annual plan, down from $21. The offer runs July 1 through December 31, 2026, covers the first year only, and is limited to existing Microsoft 365 customers on an eligible Business plan. Monthly billing is $25.20. Business Standard with Copilot is $23.50 and Business Premium with Copilot is $32. Business Standard itself rose to $14 on July 1.

What it changes

Your first year is cheap and your second is not. At 20 seats, $18 costs $4,320 a year and $21 costs $5,040, a $720 gap you pay from year two. A trade article put the end of the offer at September 30; Microsoft's own page says December 31. Get your quote in writing with the end date. Ask your partner which Copilot the bundles include, because their listed price is lower than the add-on.

What not to do

Do not buy seats for everyone to catch the $18; the discount only helps seats someone uses. Do not sign without the end date and the year-two price in writing. Do not pay monthly at $25.20 if you plan to keep the seat for a year. Do not assume your Business Standard renewal holds at $12.50; existing customers keep it only until their next renewal after July 1.

The tape

Jul 1Business Basic rises from $6 to $7 and Business Standard from $12.50 to $14; Premium holds at $22. Standard with Copilot ($23.50) and Premium with Copilot ($32) become permanent products. Existing customers keep old prices until renewal.
Jul 7Collab365 reports the Copilot Business cut, $21 to $18 per user per month billed yearly, and gives September 30 as the end date. Microsoft's own pricing page states December 31.
Sep 30The end date one trade article gave for the Copilot Business offer. Microsoft's pricing page lists December 31 instead, so confirm the date in your quote before you plan around either one.
Dec 31Microsoft's stated last day for the $18 first-year price. After it, Copilot Business lists at $21 on annual billing and $25.20 monthly, and the bundles keep their listed prices.
NextYour own renewal date decides what happens to your price. Pull the date and the seat count from your Microsoft admin center this week, before anyone calls you with a quote.

Year two is where the price sits

Promotional pricing is built on one assumption: you will not look again. The $18 seat is a first-year price tied to an annual commitment. It applies only to existing Microsoft 365 business customers, and the page says Microsoft can cancel or change the offer at any time. When the year ends, the line on your invoice goes to the standard $21, and a five-seat team that stopped using it still pays $1,260 a year. The discount buys you a year of evidence. It does not buy you a better product.

Treat the discount as a trial with a deadline. Before December 31, pick the people who will use Copilot weekly and buy only those seats. Ask your Microsoft partner for two quotes: the add-on, and the bundle that pairs Copilot with Business Standard or Premium. Put a calendar reminder 60 days before the anniversary to check who used it. The trade-off is simple. Fewer seats means a smaller discount in dollars, but you stop paying for chairs nobody sits in at month thirteen.

The play

  1. You, this week: open the Microsoft 365 admin center, go to Billing, and write down your plan, seat count and renewal date for each subscription.
  2. List the people who will open Copilot weekly. Buy those seats only.
  3. Email your Microsoft partner or reseller: "Please quote Copilot Business for [number] seats and the Business Standard with Copilot bundle for the same seats. Show the first-year and second-year price, the offer end date and which Copilot each one includes."
  4. Run The Year Two Bill on this page with your total seats and the seats that will really be used. It prices both years.
  5. Set two calendar reminders: December 15 to place the order if you are buying, and 60 days before the anniversary to review usage.
  6. Paste into ChatGPT or Claude: "Here are two Microsoft quotes: [paste]. Compare the cost per user per year for years one and two, and list what is different between them in plain words."

04 / Skip itBrief · 45 sec

Skip company-wide Copilot seats. Buy three and see who uses them.

What moved

A Recon Analytics survey of more than 150,000 U.S. customers, reported by the Wall Street Journal and summarized by Futurism, found the share of paying Copilot subscribers who preferred it as their main chatbot fell from 18.8% to 11.5% between July 2025 and January. Gemini's rose from 12.8% to 15.7%. A Citi note said some companies use about 10% of the Copilot seats they paid for. Microsoft disputed the reporting.

What it changes

A seat is a monthly bill whether anyone opens it. At Copilot Business's $21 list price, 20 seats cost $5,040 a year. If six in ten sit idle, the rate one analyst says the average Copilot company sees, $3,024 pays for nothing. The surveys are large-company data, so your own rate is unknown, and that is the point. You will know it after 30 days.

What not to do

Do not buy a seat for every employee because the demo looked good. Do not read these surveys as proof that Copilot fails; they measure large companies, and Microsoft disputes them. Do not skip the usage check at day 30. Do not renew seats on autopilot. Do not buy a bigger plan to fix low usage; train the three people who opened it.

The tape

Jul 2025Start of the Recon Analytics survey window, covering more than 150,000 U.S. customers. In July, 18.8% of paying Copilot subscribers preferred it as their primary chatbot and 12.8% preferred Gemini.
Jan 2026The same survey's January reading: 11.5% of paying Copilot subscribers prefer it as their primary chatbot, and 15.7% prefer Gemini. The Wall Street Journal reports the findings alongside a Citi research note.
Feb 7Futurism summarizes the Wall Street Journal's reporting. Citi research found some companies use only about 10% of the Copilot seats they bought. A Microsoft spokesperson disputes the reporting and cites the pace of growth.
Jul 29Microsoft's fiscal fourth quarter results report 30 million paid Copilot seats. Seats sold is not seats used, and the sources reviewed give no count of how many are in active use.
NextYour own pilot is the only usage number that matters. Start it before your next renewal, and set the day-30 review on the calendar now so the decision does not slide.

Idle seats are the real cost

The pitch for a company-wide license is that usage will follow. The data says otherwise. Larridin's Russ Fradin said that at the average company with Copilot, six or seven in ten employees use it basically zero. That comes from a podcast, with no method published, so treat it as one analyst's read. The Recon numbers are firmer: among paying subscribers, the share naming Copilot as their main chatbot dropped from 18.8% to 11.5%. Buying is a one-day decision. Usage is a daily one.

Run a 30-day pilot instead. Buy three seats for the people with the heaviest writing, spreadsheet or meeting load. Give each one a single weekly job and a log: uses per week, minutes saved, minutes spent checking. At day 30, keep a seat if the log shows at least an hour saved a week. Drop it if not. Then add seats only for people who ask for one. The trade-off is that some people wait a month. Compare that with paying for a chair nobody uses for twelve.

The play

  1. You, this week: list every Copilot or other AI seat you pay for, with the person's name and the monthly price. Add the renewal date.
  2. Pick three people with the heaviest writing, spreadsheet or meeting load. Those hold the seats; every other request waits 30 days.
  3. Give each pilot user one weekly job and paste this to start: "I am a [role]. Every week I [describe the task]. Walk me through doing it with Copilot, step by step, and tell me which parts I must check myself."
  4. Each Friday, each user logs three numbers: times used, minutes saved, minutes spent checking.
  5. Use The Seat Pilot on this page to write the 30-day plan, the log sheet and the keep or drop rule.
  6. At day 30, keep a seat only if the log shows an hour or more saved a week. Add seats for people who ask, one at a time.
Sources

Futurism: Recon Analytics survey and Citi note on Copilot usage, February 7 · TIKR: Larridin's Russ Fradin on Copilot use, and 30 million paid seats

Citi found some companies using about 10% of the seats they paid for, and Microsoft disputes the reporting. A 30-day log settles it for your company.

05 / Steal thisBrief · 45 sec

QuickBooks now writes your invoice reminders. Turn it on this week.

What moved

Intuit's fall QuickBooks release, dated October 26, adds AI-drafted invoices and payment reminders, in beta, on Essentials through Advanced. Intuit says customers get paid four days sooner on average, which is its own figure. The release also adds AI reconciliation, vendor emails that become bills, and connectors that let ChatGPT and Claude work with QuickBooks on plans from Simple Start up. Intuit notes that not every feature is live on October 26.

What it changes

Nobody owns the chase, so late invoices sit. Drafted reminders turn it into a five-minute review on Monday. Intuit's four days is a vendor average, not a promise. Your gain is real if your invoices are late because nobody asked, not because the customer disputes the work. Prices also rose for Essentials, Plus and Advanced renewals from August 1, so check your renewal quote.

What not to do

Do not send any reminder you have not read. Do not remind a customer who has a dispute open. Do not rely on a beta feature as your only follow-up; keep a Monday calendar check. Do not give an AI connector more access than invoices need; read the permissions screen. Do not skip your renewal quote; Essentials, Plus and Advanced prices rose from August 1.

The tape

Jul 2025Intuit announces a pay now button on QuickBooks invoices, meant to cut late payments, and an AI bank feed that suggests transaction categories from past patterns. PYMNTS reports it with no pricing and no payment-speed figure.
Aug 1Intuit raises monthly prices for QuickBooks Online Essentials, Plus and Advanced on renewals from this date. Free, Lite, Ledger and Simple Start are not affected. The amount depends on your subscription and renewal date.
Oct 26Intuit's fall release lists AI-drafted invoices and reminders in beta, AI reconciliation, vendor emails that become bills, and ChatGPT and Claude connectors. Intuit notes that not all features are live on release day.
NextCheck the settings and What's new pages in your QuickBooks account for the features and your actual renewal price. One accountant summary of the release says beta availability varies by product and account.

A reminder is a draft you approve

Late invoices are rarely a payment problem. They are a follow-up problem. The invoice goes out, the due date passes, and the next reminder waits for someone to remember. QuickBooks now drafts the invoice and the payment reminder for you, and Intuit says customers pay four days sooner on average. That is the company's number, and the feature is in beta, so availability varies by account. The mechanism is simple: the draft removes the blank page. Your job moves from writing to deciding whether the tone fits this customer.

Start with your oldest five unpaid invoices. Ask the drafting tool, or ChatGPT or Claude through the QuickBooks connector, to write a reminder for each one in your voice. Read each, then send. Put a three-step ladder in place: a friendly note the day after due, a firm one at day 7, a call at day 21. The trade-off is control. A reminder written in a minute can land wrong with a long-time customer, so keep your own name on anything for your top accounts.

The play

  1. You or your bookkeeper, today: in QuickBooks, run the A/R aging report and list every invoice more than 7 days past due, with amount and customer.
  2. Check Settings and What's new for the AI-drafted invoice and reminder beta on your plan, Essentials through Advanced. If it is not there yet, use steps 3 to 5 with ChatGPT or Claude instead.
  3. Paste into ChatGPT or Claude: "Write a payment reminder for an invoice of [amount], due [date], for a customer of [years] years. Friendly, two sentences, with the pay link and my phone number. Then write a firmer version for day 7."
  4. Read every draft and send it from your own email. Nothing goes out unread.
  5. Use The Reminder Ladder on this page to write the full three-step sequence for your payment terms and tone.
  6. Put 'Review A/R and send reminders' on the calendar every Monday at 9. Track days to pay for 60 days and compare.
