01 / Works nowBrief · 45 sec

Half of small business workers use AI. Almost none automate anything.

What moved

On June 17 the U.S. Chamber of Commerce Foundation and Ipsos published the Main Street AI Monitor, a May survey of 1,070 workers at companies with 2 to 499 employees. Half use AI at work. Of those, 64% mainly use it for personal productivity: drafting, summarizing, brainstorming. Only 6% use it to automate workflows with minimal human involvement. Six in 10 put the time saved into more or better work.

What it changes

The payoff at your size sits in everyday drafting, not in a build project. Among AI users who write or edit communications, 90% use AI for it. Research follows at 88%. The Chamber's separate October 2 report says 25% of small businesses see AI creating permanent jobs and 6% see it letting them cut headcount. Both reports come from a business group. Pick one drafting job per person and measure it.

What not to do

Do not buy an automation platform before one person has a weekly drafting job that works. Do not send AI-written replies to customers unread. Do not count minutes saved until you subtract the minutes spent checking. Do not treat a survey of 1,070 workers as a result for your shop; time one task yourself.

The tape

May 8Ipsos begins fieldwork for the Chamber Foundation's Main Street AI Monitor, polling 1,070 U.S. adults employed at companies with 2 to 499 employees. Fieldwork runs through May 11.
Jun 17The Chamber Foundation publishes the Main Street AI Monitor. Half of small business workers use AI, 64% of them mainly for personal productivity, 6% for workflows with minimal human involvement. 59% put saved time into more or better work.
Oct 2At an event in Detroit the Chamber's technology arm releases its Empowering Small Business report. It says AI adoption among small businesses is 66%, up from 23% in 2023, and 25% see AI creating permanent jobs against 6% reducing headcount.
NextYour own timing decides it. Time one drafting task for two weeks, checking minutes included, before you buy a second tool or any automation. Both surveys are a business group's view, not your books.

Drafting pays first, automation comes later

Personal productivity means one person, one tool, one task. It needs no integration, no IT and no contract, which is why 64% of AI users land there. Automation needs a trigger, an owner and a failure plan, and only 6% have built one. That gap is not laziness. A draft has a human reader before it goes anywhere, so an error costs a rewrite. An automated workflow sends the error to a customer. The first stage teaches your team what the tool gets wrong in your business, and that knowledge is what makes the second stage safe.

So start where the Chamber's data points. Pick the three messages each person writes most: the quote follow-up, the schedule change, the vendor reply. Give each a saved prompt with your tone and your standard terms, and have the person edit before sending. Time the task for two weeks. The trade-off is sameness: drafts that skip your edit start to sound alike, and customers notice. Keep one human edit on anything with a price, a date or a promise in it. Automate only the message you have edited the same way ten times.

The play

  1. You, today: ask each person on your team to name the three messages they write most. Put the list in one shared doc.
  2. Paste into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini: "Here are three messages I send every week: [paste examples]. Write a reusable prompt for each that keeps my tone and my standard terms, and list what a person must check before sending."
  3. Save each prompt where the tool keeps saved prompts, projects or custom instructions, so the team reuses it instead of retyping.
  4. Have each person time one message by hand for a week, then with the prompt for two weeks. Write down drafting minutes and checking minutes separately.
  5. Use The Drafting Dividend on this page to price the hours back at your loaded hourly cost.
  6. At day 30, keep the prompts that saved time. Only then look at automating the one message you edit the same way every time.
Sources

U.S. Chamber of Commerce Foundation: Main Street AI Monitor, 1,070 workers, June 17 · U.S. Chamber of Commerce: Empowering Small Business report, AI and small business jobs, October 2

A draft gets read by a person before it goes out, so a wrong sentence costs a rewrite. Automation sends the same sentence straight to a customer.

02 / BewareBrief · 45 sec

Hidden text in an email can hijack an AI agent. Limit what it can reach.

What moved

Radware researchers reported a zero-click flaw they named ShadowLeak in ChatGPT's Deep Research agent in June. In their demonstration a malicious email made the agent leak user data with no click or open by the user. OpenAI confirmed the fix on September 3. On December 24, ITPro reported OpenAI's write-up on protecting its ChatGPT Atlas browser agent, which calls prompt injection unlikely to ever be fully solved.

What it changes

That flaw is patched. The pattern is not. An agent that reads your email and can also fetch or send things can obey instructions hidden in a message from a stranger. Every connector you give it, whether Gmail, Drive or your CRM, widens what a hidden instruction can reach. OpenAI's own wording means no vendor fix closes this for you. Your control is what the agent can see and send.

What not to do

Do not connect an AI agent to the inbox that receives invoices, resumes and cold email from strangers. Do not give one agent Gmail, Drive and the CRM together. Do not let an agent send, delete or pay without a person approving it. Do not assume a patched flaw means the whole class of attack is gone. Do not let staff link personal AI accounts to work data.

The tape

JunRadware reports the ShadowLeak flaw in ChatGPT's Deep Research agent to OpenAI. In the researchers' demonstration a crafted email makes the agent leak user data with no click or open from the user, which is why it is called zero-click.
Sep 3OpenAI confirms the ShadowLeak fix, per Ynet's report of the Radware research. The patch closes that one path in that one agent. It does not change what any other agent you connect can read or send.
Dec 24ITPro reports OpenAI's write-up on hardening ChatGPT Atlas. It describes prompt injection as a serious threat for AI browsers and as a problem unlikely to ever be fully solved. Your permission settings stay your job.
NextNo patch date applies to you. Before your next agent trial, write the list of accounts it will read and name the person who approves what it sends.

Hidden instructions ride in on ordinary mail

An AI agent reads text the way you read an email, but it cannot reliably tell your instructions from an instruction written into the page. A stranger can put a line in an invoice email asking the agent to collect the customer list and send it out. The agent reading that inbox sees it as part of the job. Radware showed this working without a click. The danger is a combination of three things: untrusted text coming in, private data within reach, and a way to send something out. Remove any one of the three and the attack has no path.

Start with a connector list. For every AI tool your team uses, write down which accounts it can read and whether it can send. Give agents a separate mailbox or shared folder holding only the documents the job needs, not the owner's inbox. Turn on approval before send for anything that leaves the company. The trade-off is friction: an agent that asks first is slower. Accept that for the first 60 days, then loosen one permission at a time, and write down which one you loosened and why.

The play

  1. You, this week: list every AI tool that connects to email, Drive or your CRM, and which account each one uses.
  2. Disconnect any agent that reads the inbox receiving invoices, resumes or cold email until you have decided it needs to.
  3. Paste into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini: "Here is how we plan to use an AI agent: [describe the job]. List every account and file it needs to read, every place it can send or post, and what a hidden instruction in an incoming message could make it do. Then tell me the smallest access that still does the job."
  4. Create a separate mailbox or shared folder for the agent with only the documents that job needs.
  5. Turn on approve-before-send in each tool, and name the person who approves.
  6. Use The Access Rule on this page to write the one-page agent access policy, and give it to your team.
Sources

Ynet: Radware finds ShadowLeak, a zero-click ChatGPT agent flaw, fixed September 3 · ITPro: OpenAI on protecting ChatGPT Atlas from prompt injection, December 24

The attack needs three things at once: text from a stranger, private data within reach, and a way out. Take any one away and the hidden instruction has nowhere to go.

03 / PriceBrief · 45 sec

Copilot agents now cost extra per use. Set a spending cap.

What moved

Microsoft's usage-based billing page, updated November 3, lists services now billed in Copilot Credits: Copilot Cowork, advanced work in SharePoint and OneDrive, Copilot Code, Teams Phone Agent, the Work IQ API and Copilot Managed Runtime. Four of the seven require a Copilot license, so credits come on top of the seat. The setting that adds future metered services to a spending policy is on by default.

What it changes

A flat seat price is no longer your whole Copilot bill. One third-party guide reports that new Copilot Business purchases carry a default cap of 4,000 credits per user per month at $0.01 a credit. That is up to $40 per user on top of the seat, or $800 a month for 20 users. It is a ceiling, billed on actual use. The guide gives October 19 for direct buyers and December 1 for resellers; confirm yours.

What not to do

Do not add seats or agent features before you open Copilot, then Cost management, in the admin center. Do not leave Auto-apply new services on if you want to approve each new metered feature. Do not assume the seat price is the whole bill. Do not give every employee an agent feature before one person has run it for a month. Do not let a reseller set your limit without telling you the number.

The tape

Jul 17Microsoft's pay-as-you-go page for Copilot Chat and SharePoint agents carries this date. It says billing is disabled by default and an administrator enables it, with charges flowing through an Azure subscription.
Oct 19Per one third-party guide citing a Microsoft message center post, new direct purchases of Microsoft 365 Copilot Business begin including usage-based billing by default, with a cap of 4,000 Copilot Credits per user per month.
Oct 30Microsoft dates its usage-based billing overview October 30 and lists Copilot Cowork, advanced work in SharePoint and OneDrive, Copilot Code and Teams Phone Agent as services billed in credits. Auto-apply new services is on by default.
Dec 1Reseller purchases follow, per the third-party guide, moved from November 2. Check your own date and your limit in the admin center before then. Next year's renewal is the point to price the real total.

The seat price is now the floor

Software has billed this way for years: a flat fee for access, a meter for the part that scales. Microsoft is now applying it to its newest agent features. For you the shift is predictability. A seat price scales with headcount and stays put. A credit price scales with how much your team uses the feature, and enthusiasm is exactly what drives that number up. The first month of a popular agent is the month you are least able to forecast, and the invoice arrives through your Azure subscription or your reseller, not in the place you check for the seat price.

Set three numbers before the first bill: a company-wide monthly limit, a lower per-user limit, and an alert at half of each. Microsoft's admin center puts these under Copilot, then Cost management. Turn off Auto-apply new services if you want to approve each new metered feature before it can spend. The trade-off is that people will hit a wall and ask for more, which is the point: every request tells you which job is worth paying for. Review the consumption tab monthly and raise limits only for named jobs.

The play

  1. You, this week: open the Microsoft 365 admin center, go to Copilot, then Cost management, and write down every spending policy and its limit. If you buy through a reseller, email them for the number.
  2. Set a company-wide monthly limit and a lower per-user limit. Use The Credit Cap on this page to price the worst case.
  3. Add alerts at a percentage of each limit, sent to you and to whoever pays the bill.
  4. Turn off Auto-apply new services on policies where you want to approve each new metered feature.
  5. Paste into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini: "Here is our Copilot setup: [seats, features in use]. Draft five questions for our Microsoft reseller about usage billing: default limits, who can change them, how overages are invoiced, and how to get a spending report."
  6. Review consumption monthly, and raise a limit only for a named job.
Sources

Microsoft Learn: usage-based billing and cost management for Copilot Credits · Microsoft Learn: Copilot pay-as-you-go service overview · Origami: default usage-based billing for Copilot Business, dates and 4,000-credit cap

A seat is billed whether anyone logs in. A credit is billed only when someone does, so your busiest month is also your most expensive.

04 / Skip itBrief · 45 sec

Skip AI vendors that promise income. The FTC went after one.

What moved

On March 24 the FTC announced a settlement with Air AI, five related companies and three owners, in a case filed in August 2025. The FTC alleges they falsely claimed buyers would likely earn substantial income and had refund or buy-back guarantees. The proposed order, filed in Arizona, carries an $18 million judgment, largely suspended because they cannot pay it, and bans them from selling business opportunities.

What it changes

The FTC says Air AI misled many entrepreneurs and small businesses, and CFO Dive reports it collected roughly $19 million from customers. The complaint alleges it also skipped the disclosure documents and earnings claims statements the Business Opportunity Rule requires. You will hear this pitch from other sellers: buy the package, income follows. A vendor that guarantees a return but will not put it in the contract is selling the claim, not the product.

What not to do

Do not pay for an AI package whose pitch leads with income you will earn. Do not accept a verbal refund promise; get it in the contract. Do not pay a large fee before you have called a customer of the vendor yourself. Do not skip the written earnings statement; a seller who will not give one has told you something. Do not treat the vendor's best case as the typical case.

The tape

Feb 2023From at least this month, the FTC alleges, Air AI and its owners falsely claimed buyers would earn substantial income and misrepresented refund policies, buy-back guarantees and the risk of losing money.
Aug 2025The FTC files its complaint in the District of Arizona against Air AI, five related companies and owners Caleb Maddix, Ryan O'Donnell and Thomas Lancer, citing the Telemarketing Sales Rule and the Business Opportunity Rule.
Mar 24The FTC announces the settlement after a 2-0 vote. The proposed order has an $18 million judgment, largely suspended, $50,000 payable for consumer relief, and a ban on marketing business opportunities.
NextA stipulated order has the force of law once a district court judge approves and signs it. Until you hold a vendor's earnings promise in writing, treat it as unproven.

What a real AI vendor puts in writing

Every genuine AI tool has a price, a scope and a way out. Air AI, per the FTC, sold an income story instead. The tell is the order of the pitch. A tool you can use leads with what it does and what it costs per month. An income pitch leads with what you will make, and the product comes second. According to CFO Dive, the complaint says it told customers they would earn back tens of thousands of dollars within 30 days. When the first number you hear is revenue, the product has not earned the right to be believed.

Run every AI purchase through five questions. What does it do on day one, in your business? What is the monthly cost after the trial? What is the refund term, in the contract? Which customer your size can you call? What happens to your data and your logins if you cancel? Then buy one paid month, not a year. The trade-off is a higher monthly price and a lost discount. Take that deal; it is cheaper than a year you cannot get back.

The play

  1. You, today: list every AI product you pay for or have been pitched this year, with the monthly cost and the promise made.
  2. For each one, find the promise in writing: the contract, the proposal or the website. A claim that exists only on a call does not count.
  3. Paste into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini: "Here is a vendor pitch and contract for an AI product: [paste]. List every claim about revenue, savings or results, say which are in the contract and which are only in the pitch, and list the refund and cancellation terms."
  4. Call two customers of the vendor at your size. Ask what the tool did in the first month and what they pay now.
  5. Use The Vendor Test on this page to write the questions, and send them before you sign anything.
  6. Buy one month, not a year, and set a day-30 check against the number the vendor promised.
Sources

FTC: Air AI and its owners will be banned from marketing business opportunities, March 24 · CFO Dive: AI startup settles FTC deception charges, March 26

A real product can be described by what it does on a Tuesday. A pitch that can only describe your future revenue is selling the story, not the software.

05 / Steal thisBrief · 45 sec

Claude's small business plugin writes your Monday brief. Nothing sends without you.

What moved

Anthropic launched Claude for Small Business on May 13, built on Claude Cowork, with 15 prepackaged skills and connectors including QuickBooks, PayPal, HubSpot and Google Workspace, per SiliconANGLE. Anthropic's tutorial lists workflows including /monday-brief, /speed-to-lead and /report-pack, and skills for inbox, invoice chasing and contract review. It needs a Pro, Max, Ultra, Team or Enterprise plan.

What it changes

This is a standing Monday meeting you run in one request. It reads tools such as Gmail and QuickBooks and puts what needs you on one page. Anthropic's tutorial says skills draft, propose and stage work, and nothing sends, posts or pays until you approve. Claude reads only what your account permissions allow. The tutorial lists no separate price, only the eligible plans, so check what your plan costs.

What not to do

Do not connect every tool on day one; start with your inbox and accounting. Do not approve a drafted email you have not read. Do not let it set prices, sign anything or send numbers to your accountant unreviewed; Anthropic leaves those decisions with you. Do not treat the brief as your books; check one number against QuickBooks every week.

The tape

May 13Anthropic launches Claude for Small Business on Claude Cowork. SiliconANGLE reports 15 prepackaged skills and seven connectors: QuickBooks, PayPal, HubSpot, Canva, Docusign, Google Workspace and Microsoft 365.
NowAnthropic's tutorial lists the eligible plans: Pro, Max, Ultra, Team and Enterprise. Setup is Customize, then Plugins, then Discover, then Small Business. Skills draft and stage work, and nothing sends, posts or pays without approval.
Day 7Your first real test. Run /monday-brief against last week's real inbox and invoices. Check three numbers by hand and edit the questions until the page matches what you would have found yourself.
NextDay 30: keep the brief if it caught something you would have missed. Add one more connector only after a month of numbers that match. Pricing beyond your plan is not on the tutorial page.

Run the same meeting every Monday

Most owners start the week by opening five tabs. The brief replaces that with one request: what came in, what is overdue, what needs you before Wednesday. The value is not speed. It is that the same questions get asked in the same order every week, so a missed invoice or an unanswered quote shows up as a pattern instead of a surprise. Anthropic's setup step lets you ask Claude to customize the plugin for your business and save it. Change the questions to match your work: jobs scheduled, quotes older than seven days, customers who have gone quiet.

Set it up in an hour. Add the Small Business plugin under Customize, then Plugins, connect Gmail and QuickBooks, and run /monday-brief once. Read the draft against the real inbox and the real aging report. Fix the questions until the brief matches what you would have found yourself. The trade-off is trust: a brief that is 90 percent right still hides the other 10, so check one number a week against QuickBooks and connect only the tools the brief reads until you have done that for a month.

The play

  1. You, this week: confirm you have a Claude Pro, Max, Team or Enterprise plan. If not, price one seat against the hours the brief gives back.
  2. In Claude, select Customize, then Plugins, open Discover, pick Small Business and add it.
  3. Connect only Gmail and QuickBooks, or the accounting tool you actually use. Skip the rest for now.
  4. Paste: "Customize this plugin for my business. We are a [type of business] with [number] people. Each Monday I need: invoices overdue more than 14 days, quotes sent more than 7 days ago with no reply, customers who have gone quiet, and anything in my inbox that needs me this week."
  5. Run /monday-brief. Check three numbers against QuickBooks and your inbox. Edit the questions until they match your week.
  6. Use The Monday Brief on this page to write the standing instructions and approval rules in one block you can paste.
Sources

Anthropic Academy: how to install and use the Claude for Small Business plugin · SiliconANGLE: Anthropic launches Claude for Small Business, May 13

Weekly consistency beats weekly cleverness. The same five questions asked in the same order surface the overdue invoice before it becomes a write-off.
