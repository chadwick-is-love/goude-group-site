01 / The DefaultBrief · 45 sec

Your next Copilot seat arrives with the meter already running, and every new agent is approved before anyone asks you.

What moved

Microsoft posted on September 16 that from November 2, new Microsoft 365 Copilot Business licenses bought through a reseller ship with pay-as-you-go billing switched on by default. The metered services named are Copilot Cowork, Work IQ APIs and GitHub Copilot Harness. The seat itself lists at $18 a user a month on annual terms. Cowork bills in credits at about a cent each, and a heavy task runs past $7.

What it changes

The seat price used to be the ceiling on the line item. After November 2 it becomes the floor. At 150 seats, a quarter of your people running two heavy tasks a day is five figures a year that never went through a purchase decision. The default configuration allows every new pay-as-you-go agent, so what your staff can spend is set by whoever releases the next agent, not by you.

What not to do

Do not sign a Copilot purchase or renewal through your IT provider after November 2 without written spending caps in place first. Do not assume the reseller configured limits; the default is on, and the setup is automatic. Do not approve metered agents company wide because a pilot group liked them. Cap by group, review the first invoice line by line, then widen.

The tape

Jun 2026Copilot Cowork is described by partners as off by default and billed separately, with admins opting in to usage-based billing for a named group.
Sep 16Microsoft posts in its Partner Center announcements that new Copilot Business licenses sold through resellers will carry pay-as-you-go billing by default from November 2.
Sep 20Partner analysis confirms the date and notes the new default allows all new pay-as-you-go agents and services, with at least one cost management policy required.
Nov 2The default takes effect for new Copilot Business purchases, standalone and bundled, through distributors, indirect resellers and direct bill partners. Existing caps you have not written do not exist.

The purchase order stops being the control

Seat licensing had one useful property for an owner: the bill was a headcount times a price, and the controller could forecast it to the dollar. Metered agent work breaks that. Cost now tracks how many tasks people hand to the software and how heavy those tasks are, and neither number sits in any system your finance team reads. Microsoft does let an admin set a hard cap for the tenant, a group or a single user. The problem is that the cap is optional and the billing is not.

Treat the November 2 date as a contract term, not an IT setting. Before any new Copilot Business seats land, have your IT provider set a tenant ceiling and group caps, and confirm in writing who can release a new pay-as-you-go agent. Keep the ceiling low for the first ninety days and let actual usage argue for more. The trade is some friction for heavy users in exchange for knowing your number. At this size that trade is worth making every time.

The play

  1. Your controller asks the IT provider or reseller this week for a list of every Copilot Business seat you hold and when each renews.
  2. Your IT lead sets a tenant-wide monthly ceiling and separate caps for heavy-use groups in the Microsoft 365 admin center before November 2.
  3. You name one person who approves any new pay-as-you-go agent, and the reseller gets that name in writing.
  4. Write the caps, the approver and the review date on one page your provider signs with the free tool, The Meter Policy.
Sources

Microsoft Partner Center: September 2026 announcements, the November 2 default · Topedia: Copilot Business via resellers ships with pay-as-you-go preconfigured · Microsoft: Copilot plans and seat prices · Quisitive: Cowork credit pricing and spending caps

Seat pricing tied the bill to headcount. Metered credits tie it to task volume, which nobody in finance can see until the invoice lands.

02 / The HikeBrief · 45 sec

Part of your new interest rate is financing somebody else's data center, and the Fed says it is not finished.

What moved

On September 16 the Federal Reserve raised its target range a quarter point to 3.75 to 4 percent, unanimously, the first hike since July 2023. Wells Fargo moved prime from 6.75 to 7.00 percent effective September 17. Twelve of eighteen officials project another quarter point this year. Chair Kevin Warsh named AI hyperscalers raising funding among the forces pushing yields up, and the 10-year Treasury crossed 5 percent.

What it changes

Every floating dollar on your revolver or term loan just got more expensive, and variable lines typically reset within one or two billing cycles. On $10 million drawn, a quarter point is $25,000 a year, and the projected next move doubles it. The projections also show no cuts through 2027, so the hurdle rate on every capital project, including your own automation spend, went up with it.

What not to do

Do not wait for the October 27 to 28 meeting to call your banker about a swap or a fixed-rate tranche. Do not recheck covenants against last quarter's interest expense; run them at the new prime plus one more quarter point. Do not approve projects this quarter at a hurdle rate set when money was cheaper.

The tape

Jul 2023The Federal Reserve raises its target range for the last time before this September, the reference point for every hike-or-hold call since.
Sep 16The FOMC raises the federal funds target a quarter point to 3.75 to 4 percent, 12 to 0, citing strong capital investment and elevated inflation. The 10-year Treasury crosses 5 percent.
Sep 16The dot plot shows 12 of 18 officials expecting one more quarter point in 2026, four expecting a half point more, and a median that stays at 4.1 percent through 2027.
Sep 17Wells Fargo raises its prime rate to 7.00 percent from 6.75. Variable business lines and SBA 7(a) loans generally adjust within one to two billing cycles.
Oct 27The next Fed meeting runs October 27 to 28, followed by December 8 to 9 with new projections. Any fix or swap you want priced before the next move belongs before then.

Somebody else is bidding for the money

For two years the rate story was about inflation at the grocery store. This one has a new buyer in it. The largest AI companies are raising capital at a scale that competes with everyone else for the same dollars, and the Fed chair said so when he explained why long yields are rising. The equipment that buildout needs is also getting more expensive. You do not have to own a data center to pay for one. You only need a loan priced off prime.

The practical response is to decide how much floating exposure you want to carry into 2027, because the projections say rates stay here. Ask your lender for a quote to fix or swap part of the balance before October 27, and compare it against two more quarter points on what stays floating. Rerun interest coverage at the higher rate. If headroom gets thin, have that conversation with the bank now, while it is your idea.

The play

  1. Your controller pulls every floating-rate facility this week: balance drawn, spread, reset date and the covenant tests that use interest expense.
  2. Price the hike already taken and the one projected on your actual drawn balance with the free tool, The Reprice.
  3. Your CFO asks the relationship banker for a written quote to fix or swap a portion of the balance before October 27.
  4. You reset the hurdle rate for any capital project not yet approved, including automation and software builds, to the new cost of money.
Sources

Federal Reserve: September 16 FOMC statement and vote · Business Wire: Wells Fargo raises prime to 7.00 percent · Fortune: the dot plot, AI equipment costs and no cuts through 2027 · Citizens Bank: how fast variable business debt reprices

AI builders are borrowing at a scale that lifts long yields for everyone, and a floating-rate line reprices within two billing cycles of the decision.

03 / The ListenerBrief · 45 sec

The bot taking notes on your client calls may be the eavesdropper in the lawsuit, and the host clicking accept is not consent.

What moved

On August 13 a federal judge in San Jose let wiretap, California privacy and Illinois biometric claims against Otter proceed, finding the vendor plausibly collects and uses call audio for its own purposes, including training. A class action against Granola under the same theory was filed July 30. A ruling on Fireflies is pending. Damages run $5,000 per California violation and up to $10,000 under the federal Wiretap Act.

What it changes

If the vendor is a party to the call, then everyone on the line has to agree, not just your employee who invited the bot. Your sales team, recruiters and account managers are running these tools on calls with California and Illinois participants every day. The vendors' own terms put the notice duty on the user, which means the exposure sits with the company that deployed the tool, not the one that built it.

What not to do

Do not assume the meeting platform's recording banner covers a third-party bot. Do not let staff choose their own note-taker on free or personal plans. Do not keep a tool that trains on your audio because it is cheaper than the enterprise tier that does not. Pick one approved tool, get the training terms in writing, and script the consent line.

The tape

Jul 30Chamberlain v. Granola is filed in the Northern District of California under the federal wiretap law and California's privacy act, on the same theory that the vendor, not only the host, is listening.
Aug 13Judge Eumi Lee lets the core Wiretap Act, CIPA and BIPA claims against Otter proceed, dismissing some others, in the consolidated In re Otter.AI Privacy Litigation.
Aug 26Briefing closes on the Fireflies.ai motion to dismiss Illinois biometric claims. A ruling is pending.
Oct 28The first case management conference in the Granola case is scheduled. Microsoft Teams, Zoom and Google Meet have already tightened controls on third-party bots.

Consent is only as good as who is listening

California requires every party to a confidential conversation to agree before it is recorded. For years the working assumption was that the host's click satisfied that, because the host was the one recording. The Otter ruling moves the question to the vendor. If the software keeps the audio and uses it to improve its own models, it is arguably a separate listener, and the other people on the call never agreed to it. Illinois adds a separate exposure because a voiceprint can count as biometric data.

The fix is cheap relative to the damages math. Standardize on one note-taker, on a plan whose contract says your audio is not used for training. Have the bot announce itself, and have your people say one consent sentence at the top of any call with an outside party. For recruiting calls and anything involving California or Illinois participants, default to no recording unless the person agrees on the record. Then check the free accounts, because that is where the risk actually lives.

The play

  1. Your operations lead surveys sales, recruiting and account management this week for every note-taker in use, including personal and free accounts.
  2. Your CFO picks one approved tool and gets written confirmation that your audio is not used to train the vendor's models.
  3. Your sales and recruiting leads adopt one spoken consent line for every external call, and recording stays off when the other side declines.
  4. Issue the recording rule to staff on one page with the free tool, The Recording Rule.
Sources

UC Today: Otter fails to dismiss core privacy claims · RecordingLaw: the Otter ruling's reasoning and statutory damages · Barnes & Thornburg: what the Granola class action means for companies deploying note-takers

A vendor that keeps the audio to train its own models is arguably a separate listener on the line, and nobody else on the call agreed to it.

04 / The SignatureBrief · 45 sec

In California, the software that screens, schedules and disciplines your people is about to need a named human. Newsom has nine days.

What moved

Governor Newsom has until September 30 to act on SB 947, which bars relying solely on automated systems to discipline or fire workers, with $500 per violation and a private right of action from July 1, 2027. Separately, California's automated decision rules take effect January 1, 2027 for businesses above $26.6 million in revenue, covering hiring, pay, promotion and termination. Colorado's replacement AI law starts the same day.

What it changes

Much of this readership crosses the California revenue threshold. From January 1, any tool that screens applicants, allocates shifts, sets pay or flags discipline needs a pre-use notice, an opt-out or real human review, and four years of kept inputs and outputs. If SB 947 is signed, workers can sue over firings the software drove. Your vendors will not carry that for you.

What not to do

Do not wait for the veto decision to start the inventory; the January 1 rules apply either way. Do not accept an applicant tracking or scheduling renewal without asking what the vendor logs and for how long. Do not describe a manager clicking approve on every recommendation as human review; Colorado asks for meaningful review, and a rubber stamp is not that.

The tape

Oct 2025Newsom vetoes SB 7, the earlier No Robo Bosses bill, as too broad.
May 14Colorado signs SB 26-189, replacing its AI Act. From January 1, 2027 employers owe notice, a plain-language explanation of adverse decisions within 30 days, human review and three years of records.
Sep 5SB 947 clears the legislature, 28 to 10 in the Senate and 53 to 14 in the Assembly, with $500 per violation and a private right of action from July 1, 2027.
Sep 30Newsom's deadline to sign or veto SB 947, SB 951 on AI-driven layoff notices and AB 1883 on workplace surveillance. Bills he does not act on become law.
Jan 1 2027California's automated decision rules take effect for businesses above $26,625,000 in revenue, covering seven employment decisions, with fines of $2,663 per violation or $7,988 if intentional.

The inventory is the whole job

Most owners at this size could not list every place software makes or shapes an employment decision in their business. The applicant tracking system ranks candidates. The scheduling tool assigns hours. The performance platform flags people. Each of those is now a regulated decision point in California from January 1, and in Colorado too, with notice, explanation and record-keeping duties attached. Newsom vetoed a broader version of SB 947 last October, so a second veto is possible. The January rules do not depend on him.

Start with a register: every tool, what decision it touches, which states it covers, and who reviews the output. That list tells you where to add notices, where human review is real and where it is a rubber stamp, and which vendors need to show you their logs. Budget for counsel to review the notices, not to build the inventory; your HR lead can do that in a week. The expensive version is doing it after the first demand letter.

The play

  1. Your HR lead lists every tool that screens, schedules, pays, rates or disciplines staff, and which states each one touches, by October 15.
  2. Your controller confirms whether trailing revenue puts you over California's $26.6 million threshold.
  3. You ask each vendor on the list, in writing, what it logs, for how long, and whether it supports notices and opt-outs.
  4. Turn the list into the document counsel reviews with the free tool, The Register.
Sources

Ogletree: California employment bills await the governor's signature · Littler: California's automated decision rules, seven steps for employers · California Privacy Protection Agency: thresholds and fine amounts · Seyfarth: Colorado enacts its replacement AI law

The rules attach to the decision, not the vendor, so the company that bought the scheduling tool carries the notice, the review and the four years of records.

05 / The MigrationBrief · 45 sec

Google moved your search budget onto a setting where its AI chooses the queries. The one grading how that went is Google.

What moved

Since September 1, Google has been converting campaigns that use campaign-level broad match or automatically created assets into AI Max, aiming to finish by the end of September. Search term matching switches on by default, and for automatically created assets campaigns, so does AI-written ad text. Google cites 7 percent more conversions at similar cost. An independent study of more than 250 campaigns found cost per acquisition up a median 16 percent.

What it changes

Your ads are now showing on searches you did not choose, and the report that tells you whether that worked comes from the company selling the clicks. At $50,000 a month in search spend, a 16 percent rise in acquisition cost is roughly $96,000 a year for the same customers. The switch happened in your account whether or not anyone at your agency mentioned it.

What not to do

Do not assume your agency opted you out; the migration runs in place and the defaults favor more matching. Do not judge AI Max on Google's conversion column alone; compare cost per acquisition against your own CRM for the same weeks. Do not let AI-written ad text run on regulated claims or pricing without someone approving it.

The tape

Aug 3Google stops allowing new campaign-level broad match or automatically created assets setups across the interface, Google Ads Editor and the API.
Aug 5Affected advertisers receive Google's email notice of the automatic conversion to AI Max.
Sep 1Migration begins and runs progressively through September, with search term matching on by default and brand lists kept.
Sep 4Google confirms a small test letting exact and phrase match keywords show in AI Mode for explicit, direct intent, a second route into AI surfaces.
Sep 30Google's target to finish eligible conversions. Dynamic Search Ads follow in February 2027.

The meter and the referee are the same company

The mechanism is simple. Search term matching lets Google's model decide which queries are close enough to your keywords to buy. More matching means more impressions and more spend, and the headline 7 percent lift is Google's own measurement on its own data. Independent readings disagree: higher conversion value in some retail accounts, but at a higher cost per acquisition, and one study found invalid traffic doubling on AI Max over nine months. None of that means turn it off. It means someone other than Google has to check.

Ask your agency for a list of every campaign that was converted and the date it happened. Pull cost per acquisition from your own CRM for the four weeks before and after. Where cost rose and qualified leads did not, turn search term matching off for that campaign, or ring-fence it with brand and URL exclusions. Keep it where it earned its place. The decision should rest on numbers you own.

The play

  1. Your marketing lead asks the agency this week for every campaign converted to AI Max, with the date and which features are now on.
  2. Your controller pulls cost per acquisition from the CRM for four weeks either side of each conversion date.
  3. Put a yearly dollar figure on the cost drift at your spend with the free tool, The Drift.
  4. Where acquisition cost rose without more qualified leads, the agency turns search term matching off or adds exclusions, and reports back in two weeks.
Sources

Google Ads Developer Blog: migration dates and default settings · Google: the AI Max upgrade and the 7 percent claim · Choice OMG: independent performance and invalid traffic data · Search Engine Roundtable: exact and phrase match tested in AI Mode

Search term matching lets Google decide which queries are close enough to buy, so more matching means more spend, measured by the seller.
