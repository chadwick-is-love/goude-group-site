01 / The Cost of MoneyBrief · 45 sec

Three Fed votes to raise your borrowing cost. Price your debt for up, not down.

What moved

The Federal Reserve held its benchmark rate at 3.50 to 3.75 percent on Wednesday, and the real news is in the vote: three of the twelve committee members dissented in favor of a quarter-point increase. Inflation by the Fed's preferred gauge is running at 3.7 percent, nearly twice the target, and market commentary immediately turned to a possible hike in September.

What it changes

Every plan that assumed borrowing gets cheaper this year needs a rewrite. The line of credit, the equipment loan floating over prime, the floor plan, the credit card balance: all of it reprices upward the day the Fed moves, and banks tighten before the Fed does. A hold with three hike votes is not neutral. It is a direction.

What not to do

Do not sit on a variable rate because the fixed quote costs a quarter point more today. Do not quote long jobs at today's financing cost without an escalation term. And do not let a renewal or balloon date in the next twelve months surprise you: pull those loan documents this week.

The tape

Jul 29The Fed holds at 3.50 to 3.75 percent. Three of twelve members of the policy committee dissent in favor of a quarter-point hike. The statement barely changes; the vote does the talking.
Jul 30June inflation prints at 3.7 percent annual on the Fed's preferred gauge, core at 3.3. The monthly dip came from a brief drop in oil prices that reversed in July, so nobody at the Fed is treating it as a trend.
Jul 31Second-quarter employment cost data lands above forecast, wages accelerating. One more argument for the hike camp, delivered two days after they lost the vote.
SeptThe committee meets again in mid-September. Analysts quoted after the decision put a September hike squarely on the table, with the inflation reports between now and then deciding it.

Why a hold can still cost you money

Small-business debt mostly floats. Lines of credit, SBA 7(a) loans, equipment notes and cards are priced off prime, and prime moves the day the Fed does. When the last three years trained everyone to wait for cuts, a committee with three members voting to hike is a change in the weather, not a detail. Lenders read the same vote you do, and they reprice risk on renewals before any official move.

The move is not panic refinancing. It is knowing your number. If a half-point hike costs you a rounding error, carry on. If it eats a real slice of your margin, that is worth one conversation with your banker this week, while a fixed conversion is a choice rather than a scramble. The tool on the right prices the risk in about ten seconds.

The play

  1. List every dollar of debt that floats: line of credit, equipment notes, cards you carry a balance on, anything priced off prime.
  2. Run the balance through the tool on the right. It prices a quarter-point and a half-point hike in dollars per year.
  3. If the number is real money, ask your banker for a written fixed-rate conversion quote this week. You are buying certainty while it is cheap.
  4. Anything renewing or ballooning in the next twelve months: start that conversation now. Every renewal after a September hike is a more expensive renewal.

02 / LaborBrief · 45 sec

Your crew got the raise and still fell behind. The gap is now your turnover risk.

What moved

Friday's Employment Cost Index: total compensation rose 0.9 percent in the second quarter, above forecast. Private-sector wages are up 3.1 percent over the year, and the health benefits employers pay for are up 6.0 percent, the fastest line in the report. The number under the number: adjusted for inflation, private wages fell 0.4 percent over the same year.

What it changes

Your payroll is rising faster than planned and your people are still losing ground, both at once. A worker whose 3 percent raise trails inflation near 4 knows it at the grocery store, and that knowledge turns every competitor's job post into a live offer. This fall, comp planning is retention planning. They are the same spreadsheet.

What not to do

Do not budget next year's raises off last year's habit; the market moved. Do not treat the benefits line as fixed: at 6 percent growth it is your fastest-rising labor cost, and the renewal hits before the raise conversation does. And do not wait for the exit interview to learn a key person was underwater. Run the math first.

The tape

Jul 31The Bureau of Labor Statistics releases the second-quarter Employment Cost Index: compensation up 0.9 percent for the quarter, 3.4 percent for the year across civilian workers, 3.3 percent in private industry. Forecasters had expected less.
WagesPrivate wages and salaries up 3.1 percent over the year. Adjusted for inflation, down 0.4 percent. The average raise did not cover the average grocery run.
BenefitsPrivate-industry health benefit costs up 6.0 percent over the year, roughly double wage growth. This is the quiet line that wrecks labor budgets.
ContextJune inflation printed 3.7 percent two days earlier. Any raise below that number is a real-terms pay cut, and your best people can do that arithmetic without help.

The raise that is really a cut

Wage growth at 3.1 percent against inflation near 4 means the standard annual raise now reads as a demotion in buying power. That gap does not show up in your payroll report. It shows up eight months later as a two-week notice from the person you could least afford to lose, followed by a recruiting bill, a training gap, and the jobs that slip while the seat is empty.

The replacement math is brutally lopsided in most trades and service businesses. A meaningful raise for a proven person costs a known, budgetable number. A departure costs recruiting, onboarding, the ramp months, the overtime that covers the hole, and sometimes the customer who followed the technician out the door. The tool on the right puts both numbers side by side, because the argument ends when the numbers are visible.

The play

  1. List your five hardest-to-replace people, their last raise date, and the percent. Flag anyone below 4 percent in the last year.
  2. Run each through the tool on the right. In most cases the raise costs a fraction of the replacement.
  3. Get your health renewal quote early and shop it. At 6 percent market growth, a flat renewal is a negotiating win worth real money.
  4. Where cash is tight, raise certainty instead: a stable schedule and a written path to the next number both retain, and both are free.

03 / MaterialsBrief · 40 sec

Canadian cement and lumber cost 50 percent more on August 19. Reprice before you sign.

What moved

A 50 percent tariff on roughly $20 billion of Canadian construction materials, cement, timber and lumber among them, takes effect August 19. Steel, aluminum and copper sit outside it only because they already carry their own 50 percent duties. Construction input prices were already up 7.6 percent over the year as of June.

What it changes

Every quote that outlives August 19 is carrying tariff risk you did not price. Cement and lumber touch nearly every trade: foundations, framing, roofing, concrete flatwork, finish carpentry. Distributors reprice ahead of a known date, not after it, and a bid signed in July can be underwater by Labor Day.

What not to do

Do not hold a bid open past its price: cut quote validity to 15 days or fewer on materials-heavy work. Do not sign fixed-price contracts without an escalation clause; the tool on the right writes one. And do not assume your supplier's July price list survives the month. Call and ask what reprices on the 19th.

The tape

Jul 22The 50 percent tariff on Canadian construction materials is signed, effective August 19, covering about $20 billion in imports including cement, concrete inputs, timber and lumber.
ExcludedSteel, aluminum and copper, which already carry separate 50 percent duties, plus energy, potash and certain minerals. The exclusions are not relief; they are tariffs that already happened.
BaselineConstruction input prices in June stood 7.6 percent above a year earlier, with tariff-touched commodities showing the steepest climbs. The new duty lands on top of that base.
MechanicsThe duty is paid at the border by the importer, then moves through distributor, contractor and owner. On public work it surfaces as higher bids. On private work it surfaces as a fight over who eats the increase, unless the contract already answered that.

The clause that saves the job

Tariff risk on a fixed-price contract belongs to whoever signed last without reading. A materials escalation clause moves that risk into daylight: it names the materials, sets a threshold, requires documentation, and adjusts the price both directions. The two-way version matters. Customers sign clauses that can work in their favor, and balk at ones that only ever go up.

The other half of the defense is boring discipline: shorter quote validity, supplier price lists checked weekly through August, and a habit of asking distributors directly which SKUs reprice on the 19th. Suppliers know their exposure to the line item. Contractors who ask are quoting off the next price list. Contractors who do not are quoting off the last one, and donating the difference.

The play

  1. Pull every open quote and unsigned contract that could close after August 19. Reprice or re-date each one this week.
  2. Cut quote validity to 15 days or fewer on materials-heavy work until pricing settles.
  3. Add a materials escalation clause to your standard contract. The tool on the right writes it, two-way, with the documentation requirement built in.
  4. Ask your top three suppliers in writing which products reprice on August 19 and by how much. Their answer is your next bid sheet.

04 / The Refund WindowBrief · 50 sec

Washington owes importers $100 billion back. The ones who filed are getting paid first.

What moved

The Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs in February. As of late June, $104 billion in refunds had been authorized and $71 billion paid, with roughly $100 billion still owed, and the checks so far skew heavily toward the biggest importers. On July 15 the trade court ordered refunds on fully closed-out entries for importers who filed suit, roughly 3,700 cases, and confirmed the uncomfortable part: importers who never filed have no confirmed path yet.

What it changes

If you imported almost anything between spring 2025 and February 2026, you likely paid duties the courts have ruled unlawful, and the money does not come back on its own. Refunds run through a claim system, protest deadlines run 180 days from when Customs closes an entry, and every week of waiting quietly closes the window on older entries. Filers are being paid. Waiters are being studied.

What not to do

Do not assume your customs broker handled it; ask for your position in writing. Do not let 180-day protest deadlines lapse while you wait for a class action to save you. And do not write the money off because the paperwork looks heavy. The first step is one email, and the tool on the right writes it.

The tape

Feb 20The Supreme Court invalidates the IEEPA tariffs. Refund machinery starts grinding through Customs and Border Protection's claim system.
Jun 29$104.29 billion authorized, $71.06 billion paid with interest. The refunds cover about 60 percent of the dollars owed but only 30 percent of the entries: large importers with counsel and clean records went to the front.
Jul 15The Court of International Trade orders refunds on finally liquidated entries for importers who filed suit, with the same relief expected across roughly 3,700 pending cases. Non-filers are left, for now, with no confirmed mechanism.
This weekThe government's status report is due August 4, a settlement conference runs August 5, and a class certification hearing follows August 6. The shape of the path for everyone who never sued could be set this week.

What "finally liquidated" means in English

Liquidation is Customs closing the books on one import entry. Ninety days after that, the entry is final, and final used to mean gone. The July 15 order broke that wall for importers who had already filed suit, which is why trade lawyers spent the last two weeks telling clients to file protective actions. For everyone else, two clocks matter: the 180-day protest window on each entry, and the class-action calendar that runs through this week's hearings.

The pattern in the payment data is the story. Sixty percent of the dollars but thirty percent of the entries means the big companies are through the door while smaller importers, the 97 percent of importing firms that are small businesses, are still finding the handle. The system is not rigged so much as it is paperwork-shaped, and paperwork favors whoever has someone paid to do it. Your customs broker already has your entry data. The whole game is making them hand you your position.

The play

  1. Email your broker for a complete list of entries that carried IEEPA duties: entry number, duty paid, liquidation date, protest status. The tool on the right writes the email.
  2. Sort by liquidation date and flag anything approaching 180 days. Those protests file first.
  3. If the total is real money, ask a trade attorney about a protective filing at the trade court. That is the lane the July 15 order paid.
  4. Watch August 4 through 6. If a class path opens, your entry list is your claim, ready to go. If it does not, you lost nothing by being ready.

05 / The WalletBrief · 45 sec

Your customer keeps 2.7 cents of every dollar. Raise prices like you know it.

What moved

June's consumer numbers, released Thursday: spending up 0.3 percent, income up 0.2 percent, and the personal savings rate at 2.7 percent, which means the average customer keeps less than three cents of every after-tax dollar. Inflation at 3.7 percent is outrunning wage growth, and nearly all of the month's spending growth went to services rather than goods, $58 billion against $7 billion.

What it changes

Price increases are still possible; the margin for clumsy ones is gone. A customer with no savings buffer does not absorb a surprise 12 percent jump. They shop it, stretch the service interval, or drop the line item. What they will still pay for is a specific number, tied to a named cost, from a business they trust, with warning.

What not to do

Do not raise prices silently and let the invoice make the announcement. Do not round up while you are at it; pass through what changed and name it. And do not discount your way to volume against a stretched customer. You will keep the busyness and lose the margin.

The tape

Jul 30The Bureau of Economic Analysis reports June personal income up 0.2 percent, spending up 0.3 percent, real spending up 0.4 percent. The savings rate holds at 2.7 percent, $646 billion in total.
PricesThe PCE index sits 3.7 percent above a year ago, core at 3.3, both far above the 2 percent target. June's monthly dip came from a brief oil price drop that reversed in July.
The splitServices took $58.2 billion of the month's spending growth; goods took $7.0 billion. Customers are still buying help. They are pausing stuff.
CrosscheckFriday's wage data says the customer's raise did not cover their groceries either: real wages fell 0.4 percent over the year. Their math is your math.

The 2.7 percent customer

A savings rate under 3 percent describes a customer running close to the line, where every recurring charge gets an annual audition. That customer does not punish price increases. They punish surprise. The increase that arrives silently on an invoice reads as disrespect and gets shopped; the one that arrives early, with the cost named and one option attached, mostly gets accepted, because the customer is doing the same thing to their own customers.

The services-versus-goods split is the other half of the read. Money is flowing to businesses that fix, maintain, clean, repair and take care of things, and pausing on objects. If you sell both, lead with the service and let the product ride along. If you sell service, your renewal is defensible this fall. Defensible is not automatic; it still has to be explained.

The play

  1. Before any increase, write down the cost that moved and by how much. If you cannot name it in one sentence, you are not ready to raise.
  2. Give 30 days of notice with the reason in the first two sentences. The tool on the right writes the note.
  3. Attach one pressure valve: a smaller package, a longer interval, or a rate lock for prepayment. Retention is cheaper than reacquisition, especially now.
  4. Check your own mix. June's growth ran services over goods almost eight to one. Put the service in front of the customer first.
